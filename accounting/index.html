Question 1A — Inventories

Paper 1 · part of Question 1: Asset Management & VAT

Inventory Systems: Perpetual vs Periodic

Perpetual (Continuous) System

  • Trading stock is classified as an asset, and Cost of Sales an expense
  • Stock movements are recorded on a continual basis in the Trading Stock account
  • Better control over stock — a stock count is done and compared to the Trading Stock account, so theft/stock losses are quickly and easily detected
  • Requires an expensive outlay of equipment (computerisation/barcodes/scanners)
  • Carriage inwards costs (railage, import duties, customs duties) are charged directly to the Trading Stock account
  • Stock returns to creditors, drawings, and donations are all credited to the Trading Stock account
  • Since Cost of Sales is recorded continuously (every sale), the Cost of Sales account shows the total cost price of goods sold

Periodic System

  • There is no continuous recording of cost of sales, so there is no Cost of Sales account — cost of sales is calculated at period end
  • Stock purchases are recorded in a nominal account called Purchases, classified as an expense
  • Carriage, import duties, customs duties, railage are NOT recorded in Purchases — they get their own expense account (Carriage on purchases, Import duties, etc.)
  • Returns, allowances, donations, and drawings of stock are credited directly to the Purchases account
  • Cost effective — no outlay for expensive equipment
  • Theft/shrinkage is not easily detected — a stocktake is necessary, then the actual mark-up % achieved is compared to the target mark-up to determine if stock has gone missing
  • If stock has been stolen, closing stock will be lower than it should be and cost of sales higher — so mark-up % will differ from target
  • Suitable for businesses where it is difficult to determine the cost price of single or individual items
Cost of Sales (Periodic system)
Opening stock
+ Net purchases (Total purchases − purchases returns)
+ Carriage on purchases
+ Import duties/Customs duties/Excise duties
= Cost of goods available to sell
− Closing stock
= Cost of Sales

Valuation Methods: FIFO vs Weighted Average

First-In-First-Out (FIFO)

Assumes the oldest stock (first purchased) is sold first. Closing stock is therefore valued using the most recent purchase price(s) — work backwards from the last purchase batch until all closing units are accounted for.

Weighted Average

A single average cost per unit is recalculated using all stock available. Better suited to a homogeneous product — non-perishable, does not go out of style within a year, not batch-dependent, subject to purchase price fluctuations.

Formulas

Weighted average cost per unit = Total cost of goods available for sale ÷ Total units available for sale
FIFO closing stock value = closing units valued starting from the most recent purchase batch backwards, at each batch's own unit cost, until all closing units are accounted for
Imported stock cost (Rand) = Foreign currency price × exchange rate
+ import duties & carriage (usually a % of order value)
Average stockholding period (days stock on hand) = ½(Opening stock + Closing stock) ÷ Cost of sales × 365

Notes & Traps

  • Carriage and import duties are added to the cost of stock, not treated as a separate expense (perpetual system) — or recorded in their own dedicated expense account (periodic system)
  • Stock received free of charge increases the number of units on hand but adds nothing to total cost — pulls the weighted average cost per unit down
  • Stock stolen or lost is removed at the cost per unit applicable under whichever method the business uses (weighted average cost, or the relevant FIFO batch cost), and is expensed (Stock loss / Loss due to theft)
  • Returns reduce both units and cost — remove at the cost they were originally added at
  • A business is sometimes asked to value stock under FIFO purely for insurance purposes (to claim at the highest possible value) even though they use weighted average for their actual records — read the question carefully to see which method the specific sub-question wants

Question 1B — Fixed Assets

Paper 1 · part of Question 1: Asset Management & VAT

Definitions

  • Carrying value (Book value) — Cost minus Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Accumulated depreciation — the total depreciation charged against an asset since it was bought.
  • Diminishing balance method — depreciation calculated as a % of carrying value each year (so the depreciation amount decreases over time).
  • Production unit method — depreciation based on actual units produced by the asset, relative to its total estimated production capacity.

Formulas

Straight-line depreciation = Cost × rate % × time
Diminishing balance depreciation = (Cost − Accumulated Depreciation) × rate % × time
Production unit depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Total estimated production units × units produced this year

Notes & Traps

  • An asset disposed of during the year is depreciated only for the months owned up to the date of sale — not the full year
  • A new asset purchased during the year is depreciated only from its purchase date to year-end
  • Land & Buildings is never depreciated
  • On disposal: transfer cost and accumulated depreciation to the Asset Disposal account; the balancing figure is profit or loss on sale (to the Income Statement)
  • Trade-in allowance is treated as the "selling price" of the old asset in the Asset Disposal account, and forms part of the cost of the new asset

Question 1C — VAT

Paper 1 · part of Question 1: Asset Management & VAT

VAT Concepts

  • VAT – Value added tax. A taxation charged on the supply of goods and services by a VAT vendor. This is collected by SARS by means of e-filing and allotted for government use.
  • Standard VAT rate – 15% (increased from 14% on 1 April 2018)
  • VAT vendor – a business who earns a turnover of more than R1m per annum is required by law to register as a VAT vendor. Voluntary registration for businesses that earn below the threshold.
  • Bi-monthly submission – VAT returns are filed at the end of every second month by the 25th day of the following month at the very latest. Penalties are charged for late submission.
  • Receipt-basis submission – to assist businesses that may experience cash flow problems, they can apply to operate on the receipt basis, so VAT is only raised when the money is received or paid. Must be applied for in writing.
  • Invoice-basis submission – VAT is raised at the point the invoice is issued or received. This can have cash flow implications, as VAT may need to be paid before receipt of cash from debtors.
  • Zero-rated items – items that carry 0% VAT, aimed at helping indigent communities. Includes brown bread, milk, maize, rice, fruit, vegetables, milk powders, cooking oil, eggs, lentils. Minister of Finance can adjust this list.
  • VAT exempt items – exempt by an act of parliament from carrying VAT. Examples: interest, rates, export service, childcare services, educational services.
  • VAT inclusive – the listed price has VAT added to it (115%). VAT exclusive – the price listed does not have VAT added (100%).

Question 2 — Company Financial Statements

Paper 1

Statement of Comprehensive Income (Income Statement) — Line-Item Order

Sales → Cost of sales → Gross profit → Operating incomes → Operating expenses → Operating Profit → Interest income → Net profit before interest expense → Interest expense → Net profit before taxation → Taxation for the year → Net profit after taxation

Common Operating Income itemsCommission income · Rent income · Provision for bad debts adjustment
Common Operating Expense itemsAdvertising · Telephone · Bank charges · Directors' fees · Stationery · Bad debts · Sundry expenses · Auditors' fees · Salaries & wages · Repairs & maintenance · Stock loss · Trading stock deficit

Trade & Other Receivables Note

Net trade debtors (Debtors control less Provision for bad debts) · Prepaid expenses · Accrued income

Trade & Other Payables Note

Trade creditors · Accrued expenses · Income received in advance · Shareholders for dividends · SARS (Income Tax) · Short-term loan instalment · Bank overdraft

Ordinary Share Capital Note — Movement

Shares at beginning of the year
+ Shares issued during the year (at issue price)
− Shares repurchased during the year (at repurchase price)
= Shares at end of the year

Retained Income / Accumulated Profits Note

Balance at the beginning of the year
+ Net profit after tax
− Repurchase of shares premium (Average Share Price − repurchase price, × shares repurchased)
− Ordinary share dividends (paid + recommended)
= Balance at the end of the year

Calculation Formulas

Loan interest = Opening balance × rate % × time (split into periods if the balance changes mid-year)
Interest accrued = Total interest expense − interest actually paid
Rent with a mid-year increase = (months at old rate × old rate) + (months at new rate × new rate)
Average Share Price = Total value of shares in issue ÷ Total number of shares in issue
Debtors' allowance = Sales returns amount × 100/(100+mark-up%) — apply to the specific mark-up % given

Traps

  • Land & Buildings is never depreciated — do not carry a depreciation figure for it out of habit
  • The share repurchase entry in Retained Income uses the difference between Average Share Price and repurchase price — not the full repurchase amount
  • Items marked "Do not calculate" or "Do not total" on a note must be left as given/derived, not recalculated

Question 4 — Manufacturing

Paper 1

The Cost Flow

Raw Materials Stock → Work-In-Progress Stock → Finished Goods Stock → Cost of Sales (Income Statement)
Direct Labour + Factory Overheads feed directly into Work-In-Progress

Production Cost Statement — Structure

Direct Material Cost (Raw Materials)
+ Direct Labour Cost
= Prime Costs
+ Factory Overhead Cost
= Total Manufacturing Cost
+ Work in process at beginning of year
− Work in process at end of year
= Total Cost of Production (also seen as "Total production cost of Finished Goods")

Cost Classification

Direct / Prime Costs

Direct Material Cost / Raw Materials — material physically forming part of the product.

Direct Labour Cost / Direct Labour — wages of workers physically producing the units, including employer contributions on those workers.

Factory Overhead Cost / Factory Overheads

Indirect Labour · Indirect Materials · Rent · Rates & Taxes · Water & Electricity · Insurance · Depreciation (factory portion) · Security · Consumable Stores used

Shared-cost trapRent, water & electricity, insurance, and security are routinely split by floor space, usage ratio, or a stated percentage between factory / administration / sales. Only the factory portion enters Factory Overhead Cost.

The Four Stock Accounts

Raw Materials Stock = Opening + Purchases − Issued to Factory = Closing
Work-In-Progress Stock = Opening + Direct Material + Direct Labour + Factory Overheads − Closing = Cost of Production
Finished Goods Stock = Opening + Cost of Production − Cost of Sales = Closing
Consumable Stores on Hand = Opening + Purchases − Used = Closing

Break-Even Analysis

Variable cost per unit = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Variable Selling & Distribution per unit
Contribution per unit = Selling price − Variable cost per unit
Break-Even Point (units) = Total Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per unit
PatternBreak-even questions often work backwards — given a target break-even point, calculate the required change in fixed costs or selling price. Practice both directions.

Question 1 — Reconciliations

Paper 2 · Part A: Debtors, Part B: Creditors

Definitions

  • Age analysis — shows how long each debtor's outstanding balance has been owing, split into Current, 30+, 60+, 90+ day columns.
  • Creditor's Reconciliation Statement — reconciles the balance per the supplier's statement to the balance per our own creditors' ledger, isolating timing differences that will resolve themselves.
  • Creditor's Ledger entry — a genuine correction that must actually be posted to our books (an error or omission), as opposed to a timing difference.
  • Post-dated cheque — a cheque dated after the current period end. It cannot be recorded until its actual date arrives.

Bank Reconciliation — Structure

Balance per Bank Statement
+ Outstanding deposits (not yet reflected by bank)
− Outstanding cheques (not yet presented)
± Bank errors
= Balance per Cash Book
Post-dated chequesMust be reversed out of the Cash Book (added back to bank) and added to creditors — they cannot be treated as a valid payment for this financial year.

Notes & Traps

  • Debit orders, stop orders, bank charges, and interest on overdraft appearing on the bank statement but not yet in the Cash Book must be posted to the Cash Book first, before doing the reconciliation itself
  • Recent papers present the Creditor's Reconciliation as a two-column table — one column for entries needed in the Creditor's Ledger, one for the Creditor's Reconciliation Statement — rather than a single running total. Check which format the specific paper asks for
  • Debtors' Control / Creditors' Control account balance must agree with the total of the individual debtors'/creditors' list — differences come from posting errors, omitted transactions, or transactions posted to the wrong account

Question 2 — Company Analysis

Paper 2

Profitability

Gross profit % = Gross profit / Sales × 100
Operating profit % = Operating profit / Sales × 100
Net profit % = Net profit after tax / Sales × 100

Liquidity & Operating Efficiency

Current ratio = Current assets : Current liabilities
Acid-test ratio = (Current assets − Inventory) : Current liabilities
Period stock on hand = Average stock / Cost of sales × 365
Average debtor's collection period = Average debtors / Credit sales × 365
Average creditor's payment period = Average creditors / Credit purchases × 365
BenchmarksAcid-test ratio: 1:1 · Current ratio: 2:1 (context-dependent — always compare to prior year and industry too)

Solvency / Risk / Gearing

Debt to equity ratio = Non-current liabilities : Shareholders' equity (expressed as X:1)
Return on average capital employed (ROTCE) = Net profit before interest & tax / Average capital employed × 100

Positive gearing

ROTCE > average interest rate on loans. Borrowing benefits the company.

Negative gearing

ROTCE < average interest rate on loans. Borrowing works against the company.

Return to Individual Shareholders

Return on average shareholder's equity (ROSHE) = Net profit after tax / Average shareholders' equity × 100
Earnings per share (EPS) = Net profit after tax / Number of shares in issue
Dividends per share (DPS) = Total dividends / Number of shares in issue
Retained income per share = EPS − DPS
Net asset value (NAV) per share = Total shareholders' equity / Number of shares in issue
NAV vs Issue PriceNAV is a benchmark, not a pricing rule — directors set the issue price, which can sit above, at, or below NAV. Issuing below NAV dilutes existing shareholders' NAV per share; issuing above NAV enhances it.

Cents-to-Rand Trap

Per-share figures (NAV, EPS, DPS, market price) are often quoted in cents but must be converted to rand (÷100) for comparison or reporting.

The Three-Part Evidence Sentence

1. Calculation (quote the actual figures) → 2. Meaning (what this shows in plain terms) → 3. Judgement tied to this company's specific context, with the relevant benchmark stated.

Question 3 — Budgets

Paper 2

Cash Budget — Structure

Opening bank balance
+ Cash receipts (cash sales, collections from debtors, other income, loan proceeds, share issue proceeds)
− Cash payments (cash purchases, payments to creditors, expenses paid, drawings, asset purchases, loan repayments)
= Closing bank balance (becomes next month's opening balance)
Only cashNon-cash items (depreciation, donations of stock/assets, bad debts written off) never appear in a Cash Budget.

Debtors' Collection Schedule — Timing Logic

Figures must sit in the column for the month the cash is actually received, counted forward from the month of sale — not the month of calculation.

Build a small grid (sale month down the side, collection month across the top) before transferring figures into the schedule — this prevents timing errors.

Creditor's Payment Schedule

Same timing logic as the Debtors' Collection Schedule, applied to purchases and payments to creditors, often combined with settlement discount calculations.

Projected Income Statement vs Cash Budget

The Projected Income Statement includes all income and expenses on an accrual basis (including depreciation, bad debts) — it estimates profit, not cash. Don't confuse the two formats under time pressure.

Working Backwards From a Schedule

PatternRecent papers ask you to work backwards from a completed schedule to identify the debtor/creditor payment pattern or percentages used — divide the portion by the total credit sales/purchases for that month to find the %.
