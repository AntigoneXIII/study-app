Arithmetic Sequences & Series

Paper 1 · A — Patterns, Sequences & Series

Definitions

An arithmetic sequence (also called a linear pattern) is a sequence with a constant first difference between consecutive terms.

The constant difference is called d (the common difference):

d = T₂ − T₁ = T₃ − T₂ = Tₙ − Tₙ₋₁

If the differences between consecutive terms are not equal, the sequence is not arithmetic. Always check at least two differences to confirm.

Core formulason formula sheet

WhatFormulaVariables
General term (n-th term)Tₙ = a + (n − 1)da = first term (T₁), d = common difference, n = position number
Sum of first n terms (form 1)Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d]Use when you know a, d, and n
Sum of first n terms (form 2)Sₙ = n/2 (a + l)l = last term. Use when you know the first and last terms

Tₙ vs Sₙ — the distinction that costs markscritical

SymbolMeaningWhat it gives you
TₙThe n-th termThe value of a single specific term in the sequence (e.g. T₁₂ is the 12th term)
SₙThe sum of the first n termsThe running total when you add T₁ + T₂ + ... + Tₙ together
Common mistakeIf the question asks "find the 12th term" → use Tₙ. If it asks "find the sum of the first 12 terms" → use Sₙ. Substituting into the wrong formula is one of the most common errors on this topic, even when the algebra is done correctly.

Recovering a term from consecutive sums

If you're given Sₙ as a formula (e.g. Sₙ = 2n² + n), you can extract individual terms:

Tₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁   (for n ≥ 2)

T₁ = S₁

This works because the sum of the first n terms minus the sum of the first (n−1) terms leaves only the n-th term. The T₁ = S₁ case must be handled separately because S₀ isn't defined.

Exam patternQuestions that give you Sₙ as an expression (not Tₙ) and ask "find the general term" are testing this exact relationship. Step 1: find Tₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁. Step 2: verify that T₁ = S₁ matches. Step 3: simplify the expression for Tₙ.

Standard problem types

Type 1: Finding a specific term

Given the sequence (or a and d), substitute into Tₙ = a + (n−1)d.

Type 2: Which term has value x?

Set Tₙ = x, substitute into a + (n−1)d = x, solve for n. The answer must be a positive integer — if it comes out as a fraction or negative, that value doesn't appear in the sequence.

Type 3: Finding a and d from two known terms

If you know two terms (e.g. T₃ = 10 and T₁₀ = 31), set up two equations using Tₙ = a + (n−1)d and solve simultaneously:

  • T₃ = a + 2d = 10
  • T₁₀ = a + 9d = 31
  • Subtract: 7d = 21, so d = 3, then a = 4

Type 4: Number of terms in a finite sequence

Given first term, last term, and common difference: set Tₙ = last term and solve for n.

Type 5: Sum to n terms

Choose the correct Sₙ formula depending on what you know (a and d, or a and l). If the question says "sum of the first 20 terms," you need S₂₀ — substitute n = 20.

Type 6: How many terms must be added to exceed a total?

Set Sₙ > [target], substitute the formula, and solve the resulting inequality for n. Since n must be a positive integer, round up to the next whole number.

Proof of the sum formulaunderstand, not examined

Write out Sₙ = a + (a+d) + (a+2d) + ... + l. Write it again in reverse: Sₙ = l + (l−d) + (l−2d) + ... + a. Add the two lines term by term: every column adds to (a + l), and there are n columns, so 2Sₙ = n(a + l), giving Sₙ = n/2 (a + l). The first form Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n−1)d] follows by substituting l = a + (n−1)d.

Geometric Series & Sum to Infinity

Paper 1 · A — Patterns, Sequences & Series

Definitions

A geometric sequence (also called an exponential pattern) is a sequence with a constant ratio between consecutive terms.

r = T₂ / T₁ = T₃ / T₂ = Tₙ / Tₙ₋₁

If the ratio between consecutive terms is not constant, the sequence is not geometric.

Core formulason formula sheet

WhatFormulaCondition
General termTₙ = arₙ⁻¹—
Sum of first n termsSₙ = a(rₙ − 1) / (r − 1)r ≠ 1
Sum to infinityS∞ = a / (1 − r)−1 < r < 1

Convergence — the full conditioncritical

A geometric series converges (has a finite sum to infinity) only when:

−1 < r < 1

This means |r| < 1. The ratio must lie strictly between −1 and 1. If |r| ≥ 1, the series diverges (the partial sums grow without bound) and S∞ does not exist.

Common mistakeIt is not enough to check 0 < r < 1. A negative ratio like r = −0.5 also satisfies −1 < r < 1 and the series converges — the terms alternate in sign but still approach a finite sum. When the question gives r as an expression in x, always solve the full double inequality −1 < r(x) < 1, not just r < 1.

Standard problem types

Type 1: Finding a specific term

Substitute into Tₙ = arₙ⁻¹ directly.

Type 2: Finding a and r from two known terms

Set up two equations (e.g. T₂ = ar and T₅ = ar⁴), divide one by the other to eliminate a and solve for r, then back-substitute to find a.

Type 3: Sum of a finite geometric series

Substitute a, r, and n into Sₙ = a(rₙ − 1)/(r − 1). Be careful with the sign — if r < 1, the numerator rₙ − 1 is negative and the denominator r − 1 is also negative, so they cancel to give a positive sum.

Type 4: Convergence with a variable ratio

When the first two or three terms are expressions in x:

  1. Find r in terms of x: r = T₂/T₁
  2. Set up −1 < r(x) < 1 and solve for x
  3. Exclude any x-values that make a term zero or undefined
  4. If asked for S∞, substitute a and r (in terms of x) into a/(1−r)

Type 5: Repeating decimals to fractions

Express the decimal as a sum of a geometric series. For example, 0.272727... = 0.27 + 0.0027 + 0.000027 + ..., where a = 0.27 and r = 0.01. Then S∞ = 0.27 / (1 − 0.01) = 27/99 = 3/11.

Type 6: Simultaneous arithmetic-geometric problems

Some questions give three numbers that form both an arithmetic and geometric sequence under different conditions (e.g. "if each term is decreased by 1, the arithmetic sequence becomes geometric"). Set up both sets of conditions and solve simultaneously.

Proof of the sum formulaunderstand, not examined

Sₙ = a + ar + ar² + ... + arₙ⁻¹. Multiply both sides by r: rSₙ = ar + ar² + ar³ + ... + arₙ. Subtract: Sₙ − rSₙ = a − arₙ, so Sₙ(1 − r) = a(1 − rₙ), giving Sₙ = a(1 − rₙ)/(1 − r) or equivalently a(rₙ − 1)/(r − 1).

For S∞: as n → ∞, if |r| < 1 then rₙ → 0, so Sₙ → a(1−0)/(1−r) = a/(1−r).

Sigma Notation & Quadratic Sequences

Paper 1 · A — Patterns, Sequences & Series

Reading sigma notation

The symbol Σ means "add up." The notation:

Σ (from n = 1 to k) of f(n)

means: substitute n = 1, then n = 2, ..., up to n = k into the expression f(n), and add all the results together.

PartMeaning
Variable below Σ (e.g. n = 1)Starting value (initial substitution)
Number above Σ (e.g. k)Final value (last substitution)
Expression after ΣThe general term to be summed
Number of termstop value − bottom value + 1

Evaluating a sigma expression

  1. Identify whether the expression is arithmetic (linear in n), geometric (exponential in n), or a combination
  2. If it's purely arithmetic: find a, d, and n, then use Sₙ = n/2[2a + (n−1)d]
  3. If it's purely geometric: find a, r, and n, then use Sₙ = a(rₙ−1)/(r−1)
  4. If it's a combination (e.g. a constant added to a geometric term, or an arithmetic and geometric part added together): split the sigma into separate sums, evaluate each with the correct formula, and add the results
Common mistakeApplying a single Sₙ formula directly to a combined sigma expression (one that contains both an arithmetic and a geometric part) gives the wrong answer. You must split Σ(arithmetic + geometric) into Σ(arithmetic) + Σ(geometric) and evaluate each separately with its own formula.

Writing a series in sigma notation

  1. Identify the pattern: is the series arithmetic, geometric, or something else?
  2. Find the general term Tₙ as a function of n (or whatever index variable you choose)
  3. Determine the starting index (usually n = 1) and the final index
  4. Write: Σ (from n = start to end) of Tₙ

Examples from your notes: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = Σ(n=1 to 6) n. Also 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36 + 49 = Σ(n=1 to 7) n².

Quadratic sequences (second-difference patterns)critical

A quadratic sequence has a constant second difference (the first differences form an arithmetic sequence).

General term:

Tₙ = an² + bn + c

StepMethod
1. Find a2a = constant second difference, so a = (second difference)/2
2. Find b3a + b = first of the first differences. Solve for b.
3. Find ca + b + c = T₁ (substitute n = 1). Solve for c.
NSC 2020 methodAn alternative approach from the memos: set up three equations by substituting n = 1, 2, 3 into Tₙ = an² + bn + c using the known values of T₁, T₂, T₃, then solve the system simultaneously. Both methods give the same answer — choose whichever you find faster.

Mixed and combination sequences

Some questions interleave two separate sequences (e.g. odd-position terms follow one rule, even-position terms follow another) or define a term recursively (Tₙ = Tₙ₋₁ + Tₙ₋₂, Fibonacci-style). For these:

  • There is no single formula. Write out enough terms by hand to identify the sub-patterns.
  • If the question asks for a sum, split the series into its component sub-sequences, sum each one with the appropriate formula, and combine.
  • If the question asks for a specific term, follow the recursive rule term by term until you reach the required position.

Simple & Compound Growth, Depreciation

Paper 1 · B — Finance, Growth & Decay

Growth formulason formula sheet

TypeFormulaGraph shape
Simple interest (linear growth)A = P(1 + i · n)Straight line — constant amount added each period
Compound interest (exponential growth)A = P(1 + i)ₙExponential curve — grows faster each period because interest earns interest

P = present/principal value, A = accumulated/future value, i = interest rate per period (as a decimal), n = number of periods.

Depreciation formulason formula sheet

TypeFormulaKey difference
Straight-line (linear) depreciationA = P(1 − i · n)Depreciates by a fixed percentage of the original value each year. Value eventually reaches zero.
Reducing-balance depreciationA = P(1 − i)ₙDepreciates by a percentage of the previous year's value. Value never reaches zero (exponential decay curve).

Spotting which type from the wordingcritical

If the question says...Use
"simple interest" / "linear growth"A = P(1 + in)
"compound interest" / "compounded annually/monthly/quarterly"A = P(1 + i)ₙ
"straight-line depreciation" / "linear depreciation" / "cost method" / "constant amount"A = P(1 − in)
"reducing-balance" / "diminishing balance" / "depreciation on book value"A = P(1 − i)ₙ

Nominal vs effective interest rateson formula sheet

A nominal rate is quoted per year but compounded more often than annually (e.g. "18% p.a. compounded monthly"). The effective rate is the equivalent annual rate that produces the same accumulated amount.

Conversion formula:

(1 + i_eff) = (1 + i_nom / m)ᵐ

where m = number of compounding periods per year (12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly, 2 for half-yearly).

Before substituting into any formulaConvert the nominal rate to a rate per compounding period first: divide the annual rate by m. Then set n = total number of compounding periods (years × m). Do not use the annual percentage directly if compounding isn't annual.

Standard problem types

Type 1: Find the future/accumulated value

Given P, i, and n → substitute directly into the appropriate formula and solve for A.

Type 2: Find the original value (P)

Given A, i, and n → rearrange the formula to isolate P. For compound: P = A / (1 + i)ₙ.

Type 3: Find the rate (i)

Given P, A, and n → isolate the bracket containing i, take the n-th root, then solve for i.

Type 4: Find the time period (n)

Given P, A, and i → isolate (1 + i)ₙ, then use logarithms to solve for n: n = log(A/P) / log(1 + i).

Type 5: Multiple interest rates over different periods

If the rate changes partway through, break the problem into segments. The A from period 1 becomes the P for period 2. Work through each segment chronologically using a timeline.

Type 6: Convert between nominal and effective rates

Use (1 + i_eff) = (1 + i_nom/m)ᵐ. To go from effective → nominal, raise both sides to the power 1/m and rearrange.

Timelines — the non-negotiable tool

Draw a timeline for every finance question, even if it seems simple. Mark:

  • T₀ (the start), T₁, T₂, ..., Tₙ (at each period boundary)
  • The interest rate(s) applying to each interval
  • Any deposits, withdrawals, or rate changes at the correct position
  • What you're solving for (circle it)
Common mistakeWhen the interest rate changes (e.g. 14% for 7 years then 10% for 5 years), you cannot use a single formula with a single rate. You must calculate the value at the changeover point first, then use that as the new P for the second period.

Annuities, Sinking Funds & Loans

Paper 1 · B — Finance, Growth & Decay

Annuity formulason formula sheet

WhatFormulaWhen to use
Future value annuity (Fv)F = x[(1 + i)ₙ − 1] / iRegular deposits growing toward a future target (savings, sinking funds)
Present value annuity (Pv)P = x[1 − (1 + i)⁻ₙ] / iA lump sum borrowed now, repaid via regular instalments (home loans, car loans)

x = regular payment per period, i = interest rate per payment period, n = number of payments.

Future value annuity — saving for the futurecritical

Money is invested at regular intervals to accumulate a target amount in the future. The standard formula assumes:

  • The first payment is made one period after T₀ (not immediately)
  • The last payment is made at the very end (at Tₙ) and earns no further interest
Common mistake — extra payment at T₀If the first deposit is made immediately (at T₀) rather than one period later, this extra payment is NOT covered by the annuity formula. You must compound it separately using A = P(1+i)ₙ and add it to the annuity result. Draw the timeline to catch this.

Present value annuity — borrowing & repaying

A lump sum (the loan) is drawn now and repaid via equal instalments over time. The standard formula assumes:

  • The loan is granted at T₀
  • The first repayment starts one period later (at T₁)
  • The loan is fully amortised (paid off) after the n-th payment

Balance outstanding after k payments:

Balance = P(1 + i)ᴼ − x[(1 + i)ᴼ − 1] / i

where k = the number of payments already made. This is the present-value formula re-evaluated partway through: the loan has grown with interest for k periods, minus all the payments made (which have also grown).

Sinking fundsexam favourite

A sinking fund is a future value annuity set up to replace an asset at the end of its useful life.

Sinking fund required = Cost of new machine − Scrap value of old machine

The exam almost always has multiple steps:

  1. Find the scrap value of the old asset using reducing-balance depreciation: A = P(1 − i)ₙ
  2. Find the cost of a new asset in the future using compound growth (inflation): A = P(1 + i)ₙ
  3. Calculate the sinking fund amount = new cost − scrap value
  4. Find the monthly payment x using the future value annuity formula, solving for x
Watch the payment timingRead the question carefully to see when payments start and end. If the first payment is "immediately" and the last is at the end, you have an extra payment outside the formula (see the warning above). If payments "cease 3 months before the purchase," then n is reduced by 3 but the fund continues to earn interest for those 3 months — compound the annuity result forward.

Loan amortisation — total paid & interest paid

Total amount paid = monthly payment × number of payments = x × n

Total interest paid = total amount paid − original loan = (x × n) − P

These are frequently asked as the last part of a loan question. The interest paid is always significantly more than the original loan for long-term home loans — don't be surprised if the total paid is double or triple the loan amount.

Deferred payments & payment holidays

If payments are deferred (delayed), the loan continues to accumulate interest during the delay period. The new, larger loan amount is then used as P in the present value annuity formula.

Example: A loan is granted at T₀ but the first payment is only at T₃ (2 months deferred). The loan grows for 2 months: P_new = P(1+i)². Then use P_new with n = (original n − 2) in the Pv formula.

Axes of symmetryfrom your notes

The hyperbola y = a/(x − p) + q has two axes of symmetry (diagonal lines through the intersection of the asymptotes):

y = (x − p) + q    and    y = −(x − p) + q

These are lines with gradient +1 and −1 respectively, passing through the point (p ; q).

Recipe for sketchingfrom your notes

  1. Step 1: Sketch and label the asymptotes (vertical: x = p, horizontal: y = q)
  2. Step 2: Find and plot the y-intercept (sub x = 0)
  3. Step 3: Sketch the curve in the correct quadrants (a > 0 → Q1 & Q3 relative to asymptotes; a < 0 → Q2 & Q4)
  4. Step 4: Find and plot the x-intercept (sub y = 0) if not already found
  5. Step 5: Find the equations of the axes of symmetry

Finding the equation of a hyperbola

  1. Read p from the vertical asymptote: x = p
  2. Read q from the horizontal asymptote: y = q
  3. Substitute a known point on the curve into y = a/(x − p) + q and solve for a

Log & Inverse Functions

Paper 1 · C — Functions & Graphs

What is an inverse?

The inverse of a function is obtained by swapping x and y, then solving for y. Graphically, the inverse is the reflection of the original graph about the line y = x.

Whatever was the domain of the original becomes the range of the inverse, and vice versa.

Finding the inverse — swap-and-solvemethod

  1. Start with y = f(x)
  2. Swap x and y: x = f(y)
  3. Solve the new equation for y — this is f⁻¹(x)
  4. State any domain restriction carried over from the original function's range

Inverse of the exponential → the log functioncritical

If f(x) = bˣ, then swapping gives x = bʹ, and solving for y gives:

f⁻¹(x) = logᵤ x

The log function is defined as: y = logᵤ x  ⇔  bʹ = x

Restricting the domain for many-to-one functionsfrom your notes

A one-to-one function (like a linear or exponential) has an inverse that is automatically also a function.

A many-to-one function (like a parabola y = x²) does NOT have an inverse that is a function, because reflecting it about y = x fails the vertical line test.

To fix this, restrict the domain of the original parabola to either x ≥ p or x ≤ p (where p is the x-coordinate of the turning point). This cuts the parabola in half, making it one-to-one, so the inverse of that half is also a function.

Inverse of y = x²Swapping gives x = y², so y = ±√x. To make this a function, restrict: if the domain was x ≥ 0, the inverse is y = √x (positive root only). If x ≤ 0, the inverse is y = −√x.

f⁻¹ vs f′ — don't confuse themcritical

Common mistakef⁻¹(x) is the inverse function (swap x and y, solve for y, reflects graph about y = x). f′(x) is the derivative (gradient function from differentiation rules). These are completely different operations. Check the notation carefully before starting — the superscript −1 means inverse, the prime symbol ′ means derivative.

Factor Theorem & Cubic Equations

Paper 1 · D — Algebra, Equations & Inequalities

The factor theoremknow cold

If f(b/a) = 0, then (ax − b) is a factor of f(x)

In practice: if you substitute a value into f(x) and get zero, then (x − that value) is a factor.

Which values to try? Test the factors of the constant term (the last number in the polynomial), divided by the factors of the leading coefficient. Usually this means trying ±1, ±2, ±3, etc.

Method — factorising a cubic completelyfrom your notes

  1. Find one root by trial: list the factors of the constant term (±1, ±2, ...) and substitute each into f(x) until f(x) = 0
  2. Write the linear factor: if f(k) = 0, then (x − k) is a factor
  3. Find the quadratic factor by inspection:
    • The first term of the quadratic: divide the leading term of the cubic by x (e.g. 6x³ ÷ x = 6x²)
    • The last term of the quadratic: divide the constant of the cubic by the constant of the linear factor (e.g. +2 ÷ (−2) = −1)
    • The middle term: find by trial — check that when you expand (x − k)(ax² + ?x + c) the middle terms give the correct coefficient
  4. Factorise the quadratic (if it factorises) or apply the formula/discriminant
Inspection vs long divisionYour Kearsney notes recommend inspection (matching coefficients term by term) as usually faster and less error-prone than polynomial long division. Both give the same result.

Finding the equation of a cubic from its graph

If the three x-intercepts (roots) are given as r, s, t:

f(x) = a(x − r)(x − s)(x − t)

Substitute a known point (e.g. the y-intercept or a turning point) to solve for a.

Solving log equationsfrom your notes

Two separate steps, done in order:

Step A — Combine: use the product/quotient/power rules to condense all log terms on each side into a single log term.

Limits & First Principles

Paper 1 · E — Differential Calculus

What is a limit?

The limit of a function is the value of y that the graph approaches as x approaches a certain value from both the left and the right. If both sides agree, the limit exists.

When evaluating a limit, substitute the value directly first. If you get a defined number, that's the answer. If you get 0/0 (indeterminate), you must simplify first (factorise, cancel) and then substitute.

The derivative from first principleson formula sheet

f′(x) = lim (h→0) [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h

This gives the gradient of the tangent to f at any point x — the instantaneous rate of change.

Method — differentiating from first principlesstep by step

  1. Write f(x + h): replace every x in f(x) with (x + h) and expand fully
  2. Compute f(x + h) − f(x): subtract the original f(x). Every term without an h should cancel completely. If they don't, you've made an expansion error — go back and check
  3. Divide by h: write [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h. Factor h out of the numerator and cancel it with the h in the denominator. You can cancel because h ≠ 0 (h is approaching zero, not equal to it)
  4. Take the limit: let h → 0 in what remains. This usually means setting all remaining h terms to zero
Common mistakeIf after step 2 you still have terms without h that haven't cancelled, you've expanded f(x+h) incorrectly. The most common error is forgetting to square the cross-term: (x+h)² = x² + 2xh + h², NOT x² + h².

The golden rule for maxima and minimafrom your notes

To maximise or minimise a function f(x): find f′(x), set f′(x) = 0, and solve for x. The value(s) of x obtained can then be tested to establish whether they yield a maximum or minimum.

Method — optimisation word problemsstep by step

  2. Step 2 — Use the constraint: use any given relationship (fixed perimeter, fixed volume, fixed total length, etc.) to eliminate one variable, so the formula becomes a function of a single variable.

  5. Step 5 — Substitute back: plug the answer back into the original formula to find the actual maximum or minimum value. Check it makes sense (e.g. a length can't be negative, a time can't be negative).

Venn Diagrams & Addition Rule

Basic probabilityknow cold

P(E) = n(E) / n(S)

n(E) = number of outcomes in event E, n(S) = total number of outcomes in the sample space.

Every probability is a number between 0 and 1 (inclusive). P(E) = 0 means impossible, P(E) = 1 means certain.

The four core rulesknow cold

RuleFormulaWhen it applies
Addition rule (general, for any two events)P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)Always valid. The subtraction avoids double-counting the overlap.
Mutually exclusive eventsP(A and B) = 0, so P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)When A and B cannot happen at the same time (no overlap)
Complementary eventsP(A′) = 1 − P(A)P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Useful when it's easier to calculate the complement.

Venn diagram — filling it in correctlycritical

Order of filling (two events A and B):

  1. Start with the intersection P(A and B) — fill this in first, since every other region depends on it
  2. A only = P(A) − P(A and B)
  3. B only = P(B) − P(A and B)
  4. Neither (outside both circles) = 1 − P(A or B)

Check: all four regions must add up to 1 (or to n(S) if working with counts instead of probabilities).

Common mistake — placing "neither"The "neither A nor B" region belongs outside both circles but inside the rectangle (the sample space S). Placing it inside a circle throws off every probability you read from the diagram.

Three-event Venn diagrams

For three events A, B, C the extended addition rule is:

P(A or B or C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A∩B) − P(A∩C) − P(B∩C) + P(A∩B∩C)

Filling order: start from the centre (the intersection of all three), then work outward — fill the pairwise intersections next, then the "only" regions, then "neither."

Independence & Dependence

Paper 1 · F — Probability

Definitionsfrom your notes

Independent events: the outcome of event A does not influence the outcome of event B. Example: tossing a coin and then rolling a die — the coin result has no effect on the die.

Dependent events: the outcome of event A does influence the outcome of event B. Example: drawing a marble from a bag without replacement — the first draw changes what's available for the second.

The product rule for independent eventsknow cold

If A and B are independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

This is both a definition and a test. If this equation holds, the events are independent. If it doesn't hold, they are dependent.

Testing for independencecritical — exam method

To test whether events A and B are independent:

  1. Calculate the LHS: P(A) × P(B)
  2. Calculate the RHS: P(A and B) — either given directly or read from a Venn diagram / contingency table
  3. Compare:
    • If LHS = RHS → events are independent
    • If LHS ≠ RHS → events are dependent (NOT independent)
Common mistake — direction of the testYou must calculate both sides separately and then compare. Do NOT assume independence and work forward from it. The equation P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B) is what you are testing, not what you are assuming. Always state explicitly: "LHS = ... , RHS = ... , LHS = RHS (or ≠), therefore independent (or dependent)."

Independence test from a contingency tablefrom your notes

Using the worked example from your Kearsney probability notes (competency test & gender):

  1. Pick two events, e.g. "Passing" and "Male"
  2. LHS = P(Passing) × P(Male) = (118/200) × (78/200)
  3. RHS = P(Passing AND Male) = 46/200
  4. Compare: if LHS = RHS, passing is independent of gender

The table makes this mechanical — just read the numbers from the correct cells and row/column totals.

Mutually exclusive vs independent — they are different thingscritical

Can they both be true?No — if A and B are mutually exclusive AND both have non-zero probability, they CANNOT be independent. (Because P(A∩B) = 0, but P(A) × P(B) > 0, so the independence equation fails.)—
Venn diagramCircles don't overlapCircles do overlap (overlap = P(A) × P(B))
Common exam trap"Are events A and B mutually exclusive?" and "Are events A and B independent?" are two different questions requiring two different tests. Some students mix up which equation to check. Mutually exclusive: check if P(A∩B) = 0. Independent: check if P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B).

Note on counting principles

Paper 1 scopeCounting principles (permutations, combinations, the fundamental counting principle) are explicitly excluded from the IEB Paper 1 probability section. Every probability problem on this paper is solvable with the addition rule, product rule, Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, or contingency tables. If you find yourself trying to use nPr or nCr, you're using the wrong approach.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Paper 1 · G — Exam Strategy

Sequences & series

  • Confusing Tₙ (a single term's value) with Sₙ (the running total) — read the question carefully
  • Checking only 0 < r < 1 for convergence instead of the full −1 < r < 1 — negative ratios can converge
  • Applying a sum formula directly to a combined sigma expression without splitting it into its separate arithmetic/geometric parts first
  • For quadratic sequences: forgetting that 2a = second difference (not a = second difference)

Finance

  • Using the annual interest rate directly when compounding is monthly — divide by 12 first, and multiply n by 12
  • Forgetting to draw a timeline, especially for sinking fund questions with irregular payment timing
  • In balance-outstanding questions, using n = total payments instead of n = payments already made
  • Mixing up straight-line and reducing-balance depreciation formulas (i × n vs exponent n)

Functions & graphs

  • Reading p with the wrong sign from a hyperbola or exponential equation — rewrite in (x − p) form first
  • Confusing f⁻¹(x) (inverse: swap x and y) with f′(x) (derivative: apply differentiation rules) — check the notation
  • Forgetting to restrict the domain when asked for the inverse of a parabola
  • In "for which values of x is f(x) ≥ g(x)" questions, reading the wrong graph as being on top

Algebra & equations

  • Forgetting to state restrictions before clearing fractions in rational equations — marks are specifically allocated for writing these down
  • In log equations, trying to eliminate the log while multiple log terms remain on one side — combine first (product/quotient/power rules), eliminate second (switch to exponential form)
  • Not checking solutions against restrictions (rational equations, surd equations, log equations) — extraneous solutions must be discarded
  • In exponential equations with k-substitution: keeping negative values of k when k = bˣ must be positive

Calculus

  • Trying to differentiate a fraction or root without rewriting in exponent form first — the #1 calculus error
  • Forgetting to classify stationary points (max vs min vs inflection) after finding where f′(x) = 0
  • In optimisation word problems, failing to use the constraint to eliminate one variable before differentiating — you need a function of a single variable
  • In first principles: expanding (x + h)² as x² + h² instead of x² + 2xh + h² (missing the cross-term)

Probability

  • Placing the "neither" region inside a circle on a Venn diagram instead of outside both circles
  • Mixing up the independence test (P(A∩B) = P(A) × P(B)) with the mutually exclusive test (P(A∩B) = 0) — they answer different questions
  • In "without replacement" tree diagrams, keeping the original fractions instead of adjusting the denominator after each draw
  • Forgetting the complement shortcut for "at least one" problems: P(at least 1) = 1 − P(none)

General exam technique

Time allocationFunctions & Graphs and Differential Calculus are typically the two largest sections on Paper 1. Strong algebra (factorising, exponents, logs) is the foundation under both — weak algebra costs you marks in two places at once.
Show every stepMethod marks are awarded throughout Paper 1. Writing "a = ___, b = ___, c = ___" before the quadratic formula, stating restrictions before clearing a rational equation, and writing the full general term before substituting n all earn marks even if the final answer goes wrong.
Check for extraneous solutionsAny equation involving square roots, fractions with variables in the denominator, or logarithms can produce extraneous solutions. Always substitute your answer(s) back into the original equation to check they're valid.

Completing the square — step by stepfrom your notes

Given x² + y² + Dx + Ey + F = 0:

  1. Group: (x² + Dx) + (y² + Ey) = −F
  2. Complete the square for x: add (D/2)² to both sides
  3. Complete the square for y: add (E/2)² to both sides
  4. Result: (x + D/2)² + (y + E/2)² = −F + (D/2)² + (E/2)²

Centre = (−D/2 , −E/2) and r² = the right-hand side (must be positive for the circle to exist).

Common mistake — the sign flipThe centre is (−D/2, −E/2) — note the negative signs. If the equation has x² + 6x, then D = 6 and the x-coordinate of the centre is −3, not +3. The signs inside the brackets of (x − a)² are opposite to what appears in the expanded form.

Circle Theorems & Converses

Paper 2 · B — Euclidean Geometry

Theorem 1 — Angle at centre = 2 × angle at circumference

The angle subtended by an arc at the centre is twice the angle subtended by the same arc at any point on the circumference (same side of the chord).

Converse: If a line segment subtends equal angles at two points on the same side, all four points are concyclic.

Exam reason"∠ at centre = 2 × ∠ at circumference"

Theorem 2 — Angles in the same segment

Angles subtended by the same chord (or arc) at the circumference, on the same side of the chord, are equal. A direct consequence of Theorem 1: both are half the same centre angle.

Exam reason"∠s in same segment"

Theorem 3 — Angle in a semi-circle = 90°

If AB is a diameter, then any angle APB at the circumference is 90°. (Special case of Theorem 1: centre angle = 180°, so circumference angle = 90°.)

Converse: If an angle subtended by a chord is 90°, that chord is a diameter.

Exam reason"∠ in semi-circle"

Theorem 4 — Opposite angles of a cyclic quad are supplementary

The opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180° (supplementary).

Related: The exterior angle of a cyclic quad equals the interior opposite angle.

Converse: If the opposite angles of a quadrilateral are supplementary, it is cyclic.

Exam reasons"opp ∠s of cyclic quad" (supplementary) or "ext ∠ of cyclic quad" (exterior = opposite interior)

Theorem 5 — Tangent perpendicular to radius

A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of contact. The angle between them is always exactly 90°.

Converse: If a line is perpendicular to a radius at a point on the circle, it is a tangent.

Exam reason"tan ⊥ radius"

Theorem 6 — Tangent-chord angle

The angle between a tangent and a chord at the point of tangency equals the angle subtended by the chord in the alternate segment.

Converse: If a line through the end of a chord makes an angle equal to the angle in the alternate segment, that line is a tangent.

Exam reason"tan-chord ∠"

Theorem 7 — Line from centre ⊥ chord bisects the chord

The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects that chord (AM = MB). Conversely, the line from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to the chord.

Exam reason"line from centre ⊥ chord" (bisects) or "line from centre to midpt of chord" (perpendicular)

Proportionality & Similarity

Paper 2 · B — Euclidean Geometry

When are two triangles similar?from your notes

Two triangles are similar (written ΔABC ||| ΔDEF) if either:

  • They are equiangular (AAA) — all three pairs of corresponding angles are equal, OR
  • Their corresponding sides are in the same proportion

For triangles (and only triangles), either condition implies the other. You only need to show two pairs of equal angles (the third follows from the angle sum = 180°).

Vertex order mattersWrite the similarity statement with vertices in matching order: ΔABC ||| ΔDEF means ∠A = ∠D, ∠B = ∠E, ∠C = ∠F, and AB/DE = BC/EF = AC/DF. Getting the order wrong scrambles all the proportions.

Proportion theorem (line ∥ to one side of a triangle)

If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle, it divides the other two sides proportionally:

AD/DB = AE/EC

Converse: If a line divides two sides of a triangle proportionally, it is parallel to the third side.

Exam reason"line ∥ one side of Δ" (for the split) or "converse: proportion theorem" (when proving a line is parallel)

Midpoint theoremfrom your notes

The midpoint theorem is a special case of the proportion theorem. If D and E are the midpoints of sides AB and AC respectively, then:

DE ∥ BC   and   DE = ½ BC

In other words: the line joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half its length.

Converse: If a line is drawn through the midpoint of one side of a triangle, parallel to a second side, it bisects the third side.

Exam reason"midpoint theorem" (for parallel + half length) or "converse: midpoint theorem" (when proving a point is a midpoint)
When to use whichIf the question gives you midpoints → use the midpoint theorem directly. If the question gives you a general parallel line (not through midpoints) → use the proportion theorem. The midpoint theorem gives you both parallelism AND a length relationship, while the proportion theorem gives you only the ratio of segments.

Perpendicular from right-angle vertex to hypotenusefrom your notes

When the perpendicular is drawn from the vertex of the right angle to the hypotenuse, it creates three similar triangles (the original and the two smaller ones). This gives three results:

Other side squaredAC² = CD × CB

Strategy for proving trig identitiesmethod

  1. Work on the more complex side only — leave the simpler side alone
  2. Convert everything to sin and cos (replace tan using tan = sin/cos)
  3. Look for: common factors to factorise, the Pythagorean identity sin² + cos² = 1, or a difference of squares a² − b² = (a+b)(a−b)
  4. Look for compound/double angle patterns to expand or condense
  5. Never cross the equals sign — reduce LHS and RHS independently until they match

2D & 3D Trigonometry Applications

Paper 2 · D — Trigonometry

These are not new formulas — they are problem-solving

2D and 3D trig questions apply the sine rule, cosine rule, area rule and SOH CAH TOA across more than one triangle. The skill being tested is identifying which triangle to work in first and how to chain results between triangles.

3D problem setup

Strategy for 3D problemsfrom your notes

  1. Identify the vertical triangle — contains the height/pole/tower and the angle of elevation or depression. This is usually a right-angled triangle (SOH CAH TOA applies).
  2. Identify the horizontal triangle — lies flat on the ground, connecting the base of the pole to the observation points. This is usually NOT right-angled (sine/cosine rule needed).
  3. Find the common side — the side shared between the two triangles (usually the ground distance from the base of the pole to an observer, e.g. BD).
  4. Solve the horizontal triangle first using the sine or cosine rule with the given ground-level angles and distances to find the common side.
  5. Then use the common side in the vertical right-angled triangle to find the height or angle.

Angles of elevation and depression

TermMeasured fromDirection
Angle of elevationThe horizontalUpward
Angle of depressionThe horizontalDownward

The angle of depression from T equals the angle of elevation from D (alternate angles, since both horizontals are parallel). This fact is used constantly in exam questions.

The sin(x + y) pattern in "show that" questionsfrom your notes

In almost every 3D "show that" question, the final expression contains sin(x + y) or cos(x − y) in the denominator. This comes from:

  1. The third angle of the horizontal triangle is 180° − (x + y)
  2. Applying the sine rule with this angle
  3. Using the reduction formula: sin(180° − (x + y)) = sin(x + y)

Expect this pattern. When the examiner's answer has sin(x + y) in the denominator, the sine rule with the third angle is almost certainly the route.

A data value is an outlier if it falls outside the "fences":

Lower fence = Q1 − 1,5 × IQR     Upper fence = Q3 + 1,5 × IQR

Any value below the lower fence or above the upper fence is classified as an outlier. On a box-and-whisker plot, outliers are shown as individual dots beyond the whiskers, and the whisker stops at the last non-outlier value.

Common mistakeThe lower fence formula uses Q1 minus 1,5 × IQR. A common slip is to add instead of subtract, which gives a fence inside the data rather than below it.

How to determine skewness in an exammethod

Method 1 — Mean vs Median (numeric test)

  • Calculate (mean − median)
  • Result ≈ 0 → symmetric
  • Result > 0 (mean is greater) → positively skewed
  • Result < 0 (mean is less) → negatively skewed

Method 2 — Box-and-whisker plot (visual test)

  • Look at where the median line sits inside the box
  • Look at the relative lengths of the two whiskers
  • The longer whisker/tail points in the direction of the skew

Method 3 — Histogram shape

  • Longer tail on the right → positively skewed
  • Longer tail on the left → negatively skewed
  • Roughly mirror-symmetric → symmetric

Comparing two data setsexam favourite

When asked to "compare," you must comment on at least two of:

  1. Central tendency: which set has a higher median (or mean)?
  2. Spread: which has a larger IQR or range? A larger IQR means the middle 50% is more spread out.
  3. Skewness: is one more skewed than the other? In which direction?
  4. Outliers: does either set have outliers?
Common mistakeStating only "Set A has a higher median" is not a complete comparison. The examiners want both central tendency AND spread discussed. Reporting only one loses marks.

Standard Deviation & Variance

Paper 2 · E — Statistics / Data Handling

Formulason formula sheet

Variance:

σ² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / n

Standard deviation:

σ = √[ Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / n ]

σ measures how far, on average, each data point sits from the mean. Small σ = tightly clustered. Large σ = widely spread.

Manual calculation — table methodfrom your notes

Calculator shortcutYour calculator's STAT mode gives σ directly (usually labelled σx or σn). In an exam, show the table if the question says "calculate" or "determine," but use the calculator to verify.

Using σ to analyse data

The interval (x̄ − kσ ; x̄ + kσ) captures data within k standard deviations of the mean.

Common exam question: "How many data values fall within one standard deviation of the mean?" → find the interval (x̄ − σ ; x̄ + σ) and count how many values land inside it.

Grouped data standard deviationfrom your notes

For grouped (interval) data, you cannot find the exact σ. Instead:

  1. Create a midpoint column (midpoint of each class interval)
  2. Use the midpoints as the x-values and the frequencies as weights
  3. Enter into your calculator's STAT mode (frequency table mode) and read σ directly

Histograms, Ogives & Frequency Polygons

Histogram

  • Bars over each class interval, height = frequency
  • No gaps between bars (continuous data)
  • x-axis shows class boundaries, not midpoints
  • Each bar's area is proportional to the frequency of that class

Frequency polygon

Plot the frequency at the midpoint of each class interval, then join with straight lines. Close the polygon by connecting to the x-axis at the midpoints of the empty intervals before and after the data. A frequency polygon can be drawn over a histogram — points sit at the top-centre of each bar.

  • Plot cumulative frequency against the upper boundary of each class interval
  • Join points with a smooth S-shaped curve
  • To read the median: draw a horizontal line at 50% of total frequency, meet the curve, drop to x-axis
  • Same method for Q1 (at 25%) and Q3 (at 75%)

Common exam trapThe question gives data for x from 10 to 80, then asks you to predict y when x = 95. You must identify this as extrapolation and comment that the prediction is unreliable because it falls outside the data range. Marks are specifically allocated for this commentary.

Effect of outliers on regression

If an outlier is added or removed:

  • The mean shifts toward or away from the outlier
  • The standard deviation changes (increases with outlier, decreases without)
  • |r| typically gets weaker if the outlier doesn't follow the trend, stronger if it does
  • The gradient (b) and y-intercept (a) of the regression line change

The median is resistant to outliers — it changes very little or not at all.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Paper 2 · F — Exam Strategy

Analytical geometry

  • Forgetting to complete the square when a circle is given in expanded form — you can't read the centre/radius without it
  • Reading the wrong sign for the centre from (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r² — the centre is (a, b), not (−a, −b)
  • Negative gradient → obtuse angle of inclination: you must add 180° to the calculator output of tan⁻¹
  • Using the negative of the gradient instead of the negative reciprocal for perpendicular lines

Euclidean geometry

  • Missing reasons: every angle calculation needs a reason in brackets. "∠ABC = 40°" with no reason is often worth 0.
  • Confusing "angles in the same segment" with "angle at centre = 2× circumference" — check which one you actually used
  • Assuming a line is a diameter without justification — you must either be told or prove it
  • Not writing "converse:" before a reason when using a theorem backwards (e.g. proving concyclicity)
  • Getting the vertex order wrong in a similarity statement — ΔABC ||| ΔDEF means A matches D, B matches E, C matches F

Trigonometry

  • Mixing up sine rule (SSA/AAS) and cosine rule (SAS/SSS) — the cosine rule needs the included angle
  • Forgetting the ± in the cos general solution, or forgetting the second branch (180°−β) for sine
  • Dropping the "+k·360°" (or k·180° for tan) entirely — a general solution without it is only a specific solution
  • In identity proofs: crossing the equals sign (working on both sides at once instead of independently)
  • In 3D problems: trying to solve everything in one triangle instead of working the horizontal triangle first to find the common side

Statistics / data handling

  • Skewness direction: positively skewed = tail to the right = mean > median. Negatively skewed = tail to the left = mean < median. The name refers to the tail direction.
  • Sign error in the outlier formula — it's Q1 minus 1,5×IQR for the lower bound
  • Plotting ogive points against the midpoint instead of the upper boundary
  • Confusing interpolation and extrapolation, or forgetting to comment on reliability
  • Reporting only the mean when asked to "compare" two data sets — standard deviation (or IQR) must also be discussed
  • Describing correlation as just "strong" without specifying positive or negative

General exam technique

Trigonometry is the biggest sectionTrig typically makes up about a third of Paper 2. Don't let geometry riders eat disproportionate time — flag and move on, come back if time allows.
Bookwork firstThe 4 proofs are fixed, known content (not problem-solving). Do them first if they appear — fastest marks on the paper if memorised, and frees mental energy for the riders.
Show every reasonIn geometry, every angle step needs a reason in brackets. In statistics, every comparison needs both central tendency and spread. In trig general solutions, every answer needs +k·360° or +k·180°. These "completeness" marks are the easiest to earn and the easiest to throw away by rushing.
