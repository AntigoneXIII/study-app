Paper 1 · Language

Paper 1 at a glance

Paper 1 tests how well you can read and manipulate language under pressure — not how much literature you've memorised. Every question type below rewards the same underlying skill: precise, evidence-based reading.

The four question types

SectionWhat it actually testsYour job
Visual literacyReading a cartoon, advertisement or news image as a constructed, persuasive textName the technique, link it to the intended effect on the viewer
ComprehensionClose reading of an unseen passageAnswer only what was asked, lift and interpret evidence, never summarise the whole passage
SummaryCompressing a passage into a fixed number of wordsExtract main ideas only, in your own words, in the word limit
Language structures & conventionsGrammar, punctuation, register, figures of speech, sound devices, common errorsIdentify the device or error and explain its effect precisely

The mindset that separates a good answer from a great one

Every marker is trained to look for one thing above all else: does the answer show understanding, or does it just show that you read the words? A quotation with no comment is a summary. A quotation with "this shows that the writer feels sad" is a start. A quotation linked precisely to technique, context and effect is what earns full marks.

Exam tip The universal answer formula for Paper 1: identify → quote/refer → explain effect. Never stop after "identify." An unexplained identification (e.g. "this is a metaphor") earns little to nothing on its own.
Common mistake Writing "this creates imagery" or "this shows figurative language" as if that were an explanation. It isn't — it just relabels the quote. Always finish the sentence: imagery of what, creating what effect, on whom?

Required phrasing markers

IEB markers are trained to look for certain sentence shapes. Building your answers around these patterns makes it far harder to lose easy marks on structure alone.

Question asks for…Use this sentence frame
Effect / reason questions"The word/phrase '___' suggests/implies/creates the effect of ___, because ___."
Diction questions"The connotation of the word '___' is ___, which suggests ___."
Tone questions"The writer's tone is ___, established through ___."
Comparison questions"Whereas Text A ___, Text B ___, suggesting that ___."
Paper 1 · Visual Literacy

Reading cartoons, adverts & news images

A visual text is never neutral. Every choice — what's included, what's left out, size, placement, colour, caption — was made deliberately to produce a reaction in you. Your job is to name the choice and explain the reaction.

Core reading framework

StepAsk
1. IntentionWhat does the creator want me to think, feel, or do after seeing this?
2. AudienceWho is this aimed at, and how do I know (setting, language level, cultural references)?
3. TechniqueWhat visual or verbal device is producing the effect (see tables below)?
4. EffectWhat emotion or conclusion does the technique push the viewer toward?
Key terms — Intention & tone Intention is the writer/artist's purpose and their attitude toward both subject and reader. Tone is the attitude that intention produces on the page — and the two must match: a persuasive intention almost always carries a persuasive or urgent tone.

Words examiners use for intention

advertise · advocate · alarm · amuse · appeal · argue · attack · convince · criticise · debate · deceive · educate · entice · flatter · humiliate · idolise · inform · instruct · mystify · persuade · praise · promote · provoke · reform · satirise · suppress · teach · urge · vilify · warn

Visual (cartoon-specific) techniques

DeviceWhat to look forTypical effect
CaricatureExaggerated physical features of a real personMocks, undermines authority, invites ridicule
SymbolismAn object standing in for a bigger idea (e.g. a dove for peace)Compresses a complex idea into one instantly readable image
JuxtapositionTwo contrasting images or figures placed side by sideForces a comparison the viewer draws themselves
Size & proportionOne figure/object drawn disproportionately large or smallSignals power, importance, or vulnerability
Facial expression & body languageExaggerated emotion on a figure's face or postureDirects the viewer's emotional response before they've read a word
Captions / speech bubblesText placed inside the imageAnchors the meaning so the image can't be misread
Framing / compositionWhat's centred, what's cropped out, foreground vs backgroundControls what the viewer notices first and what they never see

Propaganda & persuasive techniques (adverts, speeches, opinion pieces)

TechniqueDefinitionExample
Name-calling / stereotypingNegative, emotive labels used to turn the audience against someone without evidence"these thugs," "the enemy"
Plain folks appealPresenting a person or product as ordinary and relatableA politician shown in casual clothes with their dog
BandwagonImplying "everyone" already agrees or already owns it"Join the thousands who've already switched"
Testimonial / appeal to authorityA famous or trusted figure endorses the product or viewA celebrity or expert fronting a campaign
Hidden fears / conditional threatSuggests the product/idea protects you from something unpleasant"If you don't act now, ___ will happen"
Snob appealAssociation with the product makes you special or superiorLuxury branding, exclusivity language
Selective facts & statisticsOnly the figures that support the argument are shown"4 out of 5 dentists recommend…"
Unfinished comparisonA comparison is implied but never completed"This soap cleans better!" — better than what?
Rule of threeIdeas grouped in threes for rhythm and persuasive weight"Simpler. Better. Faster."
Weasel wordsVague qualifiers that sound like promises but commit to nothing"up to," "may help," "real"
Rhetorical questionA question asked to make a point, not to get an answer"Is this the future we want?"
AnecdoteA single emotive personal story used to represent a whole issueOne family's story standing in for a national crisis
Exam tip When asked "how is the viewer persuaded," always name the technique and connect it to the product/argument's specific claim. "Bandwagon appeal" alone is worth little — "the advert uses bandwagon appeal by suggesting most people already own the product, pressuring the viewer not to be left out" is the full-mark version.
Common mistake Describing the image ("there is a man standing next to a car") instead of analysing it. Markers can see the image — they're marking your interpretation of it, not your description.

Sample worked question

Q: Refer to the advertisement. Identify ONE persuasive technique used and explain its effect. (2) Model answer

Technique: Testimonial/appeal to authority — the advert features a well-known athlete endorsing the product. Effect: Because the audience already trusts and admires this figure, their association with the product transfers that trust onto the brand, making the viewer more likely to believe the product is effective without needing independent evidence.

Paper 1 · Comprehension

Comprehension strategy

Comprehension is not a memory test — the passage is right there. It's a precision test: can you find exactly what was asked, and explain it in a way that proves you understood it?

The reading sequence

StepActionWhy
1Read all the questions first, before the passageTells you what to hunt for while reading, so the first read is already purposeful
2Read the passage once for overall meaningBuilds context so later close-reading isn't done blind
3Answer in passage order, not question order if they jump aroundAvoids re-reading the same sections repeatedly
4Underline the exact word/phrase the question is targeting before you writeKeeps the answer anchored to evidence rather than general impression

Matching your answer to the mark allocation

MarksWhat's expected
1 markA single word, fact, or short direct quotation — no explanation needed
2 marksOne point plus a brief explanation, or two separate points
3+ marksA developed explanation: point, evidence from the text, and a link back to the question
Key terms — question verbs Identify/name = state it directly, no explanation required. Explain = say what it means and why. Discuss = explore more than one angle or implication. Comment on = give your interpretation, supported by the text. Refer to context = use the specific paragraph/line named, not the whole passage.

Answering rules that protect marks

RuleDetail
Answer in your own words unless quoting is asked forLifting a full sentence when "in your own words" is specified forfeits the mark even if the content is correct
Stay inside the stated contextIf the question says "in paragraph 3," evidence from paragraph 7 will not be credited
Answer the actual question asked"Why" wants a reason; "how" wants a method or process — don't answer one when the other is asked
Write full sentences unless told otherwiseSingle words are only acceptable when the question is a direct 1-mark identification
Common mistake Padding an answer with everything you know about the topic instead of what was asked. Extra correct-sounding information that doesn't answer the specific question earns zero marks and wastes time.
Common mistake Quoting when the question says "in your own words," or paraphrasing so loosely when a direct quote is required that the reference can't be verified against the text.
Exam tip If a question is worth more marks than you have points for, you are almost certainly missing something the passage stated — go back and re-read the specific lines referenced rather than stretching one idea across all the marks.
Exam tip For "in your own words" questions, a useful test: if you can point to the exact words in the passage that match your answer word-for-word, you haven't paraphrased enough.
Paper 1 · Summary Writing

Summary writing technique

A summary is a compression exercise: strip a passage down to only its main ideas, rewritten in your own words, inside a fixed word limit. Every rule below exists to stop you from either copying or waffling.

The method, step by step

StepAction
1Reread the question — it usually narrows the task (e.g. "summarise the reasons given for…," not the whole passage)
2Go through the relevant section and underline only the main ideas — one per point, not supporting detail or examples
3Cross out repetition, examples, anecdotes, and descriptive padding — these never belong in a summary
4Convert each underlined idea into your own words, as a single point
5Draft in full sentences or a numbered/bulleted list, exactly as the question format specifies
6Count your words and cut to the limit — cut whole weaker points, not just words within stronger ones

What counts as a "main idea" vs what to leave out

IncludeLeave out
Core facts, arguments, causes, resultsExamples used to illustrate a point already included
Each distinct reason or stepRepeated ideas phrased differently
Information directly answering the question's focusDescriptive or emotive language, figures of speech
Direct quotations from the passage
Your own opinion or added commentary
Required format markers Always check exactly what the question demands: a numbered list of points vs continuous prose; a specific word or point limit; whether points must be in the order they appear in the passage. Markers stop counting once the limit is reached — anything after it is not read.
Common mistake Lifting phrases directly from the passage. Even changing one or two words is not "your own words" — the sentence structure has to be genuinely rebuilt, not lightly edited.
Common mistake Including an example or anecdote as if it were a main idea. If it only illustrates a point you've already summarised, it's redundant and costs you a word-limit slot that a real idea could have used.
Common mistake Going over the word/point limit "just to be safe." Markers are instructed to stop reading at the limit — every word past it is invisible, and an overlong summary often signals you haven't actually compressed the ideas.
Exam tip Write your draft slightly over the limit first, then cut — it's far easier to remove the weakest point than to stretch a too-short summary to fit.
Exam tip If asked for a specific number of points (e.g. "list FIVE reasons"), one point per line with no elaboration is safest — don't merge two ideas into one point or split one idea across two.

Nine building blocks. Every language-structures question is really asking you to identify one of these and explain what it's doing.

Nouns name a person, place or thing.

Verbs

TypeDefinition

Key terms — infinitives, participles, gerunds Infinitive: "to" + base verb (to run) — can function as noun, adjective or adverb. Participle: past (usually -ed/-en/-t) or present (-ing) verb form. Gerund: a present/past participle functioning as a noun ("Swimming is good for you"). Gerundive: a participle functioning as an adjective ("my swimming costume").
Common mistake Confusing a gerund with a gerundive. Ask what job the -ing word is doing in the sentence: if it's the subject/object of the verb, it's a gerund (noun); if it's describing another noun, it's a gerundive (adjective).

Punctuation

Punctuation exists to clarify meaning and control how a sentence should be read. Every mark below has a defined job — using the wrong one is a language error, not a style choice.

Basic marks

MarkFunction
Full stop (.)Ends a declarative sentence or completed statement; also follows many abbreviations
Question mark (?)Ends a direct question
Exclamation mark (!)Marks a sudden outcry or strong emphasis

Commas, semicolons & colons

MarkUseExample
CommaSeparates subordinate clauses/phrases from the main clause; separates 3+ list items; encloses parenthesis (in pairs); separates direct address; follows linking words like "However"My brother, who lives in Durban, is visiting us next week.
SemicolonConnects two independent clauses more closely than a full stop wouldLet's go to lunch; they have the best burgers.
ColonIntroduces a quotation, explanation, example or listShakespeare said it best: "The robbed that smiles, steals something from the thief."
Common mistake The comma splice — joining two complete sentences with only a comma ("She left, he stayed"). Use a full stop, semicolon, or a conjunction instead.
Common mistake Confusing its (possession) with it's (contraction of "it is"). "The cat needs its collar as it's one of the estate's rules."

Dashes, hyphens & brackets

MarkUseExample
Dash (–)Pause before adding emphasis; a double dash forms parenthesisShe had one goal in mind – winning.
Hyphen (-)Joins compound words or a prefix to its root; breaks a word across a lineninety-nine, ex-boyfriend
Round brackets ( )Enclose additional/parenthetical informationThe event (held annually) drew large crowds.
Square brackets [ ]Show a change made to an original quotation"[The council] approved the plan."

Apostrophes, quotation marks & ellipses

MarkUse
Apostrophe (')Contraction (I've), omission ('rock 'n' roll), possession (Thandeka's book)
Double inverted commas (" ")Direct speech
Single inverted commas (' ')Quotes within quotes; can mark irony or a word not to be taken literally; encloses titles of poems/short stories/articles
Ellipsis (…)Shows an omission within a quote, hesitation, or a trailing-off thought

Abbreviations

RuleExample
Full stop if the abbreviation doesn't end on the word's last letterProf., tsp.
No full stop if it does end on the last letterDr, Mr, Ave
No full stop for metric unitscm, km
Add "s" (no apostrophe) to pluraliseMPs
Key terms — acronym vs initialism An acronym is pronounced as one word (NASA). An initialism is pronounced letter by letter (SAA).
Exam tip When a question asks you to punctuate or correct a sentence, read it aloud in your head first — the natural pause points usually reveal exactly where a comma, dash, or full stop belongs.

Sentence structure

Phrases, clauses, sentence types, concord and tense all get tested as "identify and explain effect" questions — the same formula as everything else in this paper.

Phrases vs clauses

Key distinction A phrase is a group of words with no finite verb. A clause always contains a finite verb — a main clause can stand alone; a subordinate clause cannot.
Phrase typeFunctionExample
Noun phraseActs as subject/objectThe English grammar test was a breeze.
Adjectival phraseModifies a nounThe boy with the noticeably evil eyes was missing.
Adverbial phraseModifies the verb (place/time/manner/reason)He presented his speech with poise during the competition.
Prepositional phraseStarts with a preposition, ends with an objectI live near the beach.
Exam tip Subordinate clauses are reliably signalled by words like: who, whom, whose, when, where, which, while, although, because, since, if, unless. If a clause opens with one of these and can't stand alone as a sentence, it's subordinate.

Sentence types by construction

TypeStructureExample
SimpleOne finite verb, one ideaSophie reads a book.
CompoundTwo+ independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunctionSophie reads a book and enjoys playing with friends.
ComplexA main clause + one or more subordinate clausesSophie is reading a book because she is writing an essay on it.

Sentence types by function

TypePurposePunctuation
DeclarativeMakes a statementFull stop
ImperativeGives a command or request; implied subjectFull stop or exclamation mark
ExclamatoryExpresses strong emotionExclamation mark
InterrogativeAsks a questionQuestion mark

Loose, periodic & balanced sentences

TypeStructureEffect
LooseMain clause comes firstReads naturally, information delivered upfront
PeriodicMain clause comes lastBuilds suspense, delays the key information for emphasis
BalancedTwo clauses of equal weight/importanceCreates contrast or symmetry between two ideas
Exam tip When asked why a writer used a periodic sentence, the answer is almost always: it withholds the key information until the end, building tension or emphasis before the reveal.

Concord (subject–verb agreement) — the traps that come up every year

RuleExample
Singular subject → singular verb; plural subject → plural verbThe kids go to the mall.
"Everybody/everyone" → always singular verbEverybody goes to the mall.
In a list joined by "or," the verb agrees with the nearest nounHer teachers, her parents, or I am to blame.
"A pair of" → singular verbA pair of scissors lies on the table.
Collective noun acting as one unit → singular verbThe team is going to meet weekly.
Parenthetical info (commas/brackets) doesn't affect the verbMary, as well as her friends, is late.
Two subjects joined by "and" → plural verbJames and John are here.
Two subjects joined by "and" referring to one thing → singular verbPap and wors is my favourite meal.
"No one, nothing, somebody," etc. → singular verbNothing was left.
Common mistake Matching the verb to the word closest to it inside a parenthetical phrase instead of the true subject — "The little girls, together with their mother, is here" is wrong; the subject is "girls" (plural), so it's "are here."

Direct vs indirect (reported) speech

Direct speechIndirect speech
He says, "I am sick."He says that he is sick. (no tense shift — reporting verb is present)
They said, "We take pilates classes every day."They said that they took pilates classes every day.
They said, "We have taken pilates classes."They said that they had taken pilates classes.
She said, "I will ask for a receipt."She said that she would ask for a receipt.
Key rule — pronoun shifts First-person pronouns change to match the subject; second-person pronouns change to match the object; third-person pronouns don't change.
Common mistake Forgetting the tense shift when the reporting verb is in the past tense — the tense inside the quotation must move one step further into the past (present → past, past → past perfect).

Exam tip If a language-structures question gives you an unfamiliar word and asks for its meaning, breaking it into prefix/root/suffix first — before guessing from context — is usually faster and more reliable.
Paper 1 · Language Structures

Register & tone

Register is the degree of formality a writer chooses; tone is the attitude that choice communicates. They're always tested together, because register is one of the main tools a writer uses to build tone.

Register spectrum

RegisterFeaturesWhere it belongs
FormalProfessional/academic language, complex sentences, precise vocabulary, no contractionsEssays, reports, official letters, speeches to authority
InformalCasual language, colloquialisms, slang, contractionsConversations with friends, personal messages, dialogue

The registers that get tested by name

TermDefinitionExample
Colloquial languageInformal, conversational language specific to a region/era — never used in formal writing, but gives characters authenticity in literatureja, hi, hey, okay
JargonTechnical language specific to a professionLegal: "Your objection is overruled." Medical: "I need you to fill my script."
SlangExpressions created by social groups, not yet accepted formally — meaning often differs from the literal words"ghosted," "jol"

Emotive language, denotation & connotation

TermDefinition
Emotive languageWord choices deliberately selected to trigger an emotional reaction in the reader rather than a neutral, factual one
DenotationThe literal, dictionary definition of a word
ConnotationThe associated feeling or idea a word carries beyond its literal meaning
Key example "Sweet," "gay," and "awesome" all have connotations very different from their strict dictionary definitions — connotation is where a writer's real attitude usually hides.

Diction & intention word bank

When a question asks about the writer's intention or attitude, precise verbs earn more credit than vague ones. Useful bank: advertise, advocate, alarm, amuse, appeal, argue, attack, convince, criticise, deceive, educate, entice, flatter, humiliate, idolise, inform, instruct, mystify, persuade, praise, promote, provoke, reform, satirise, suppress, urge, vilify, warn.

Exam tip The required phrasing for register/tone questions: "The writer's register is [formal/informal], which creates a [tone] tone, evident in the word/phrase '___'." Naming register alone, without linking it to tone and effect, only earns partial credit.
Common mistake Labelling any informal word as "slang" or any technical word as "jargon" without checking the actual definition — colloquialism, slang and jargon are three distinct categories and markers do check which one actually applies.

Figures of speech

Figures of speech make a comparison or give human qualities to something non-human. Naming the device is step one — the mark is in explaining what the comparison achieves.

DeviceDefinitionExample
SimileCompares two unlike things using "like" or "as"The Internet is like a window to the world.
MetaphorDirect comparison between two unlike things — no "like"/"as"The Internet is a window to the world.
Extended metaphorA metaphor stretched across several lines or a whole passage, with every image relating back to it"the embers of patriotism… we will tend them and fan them until they burn brightly"
PersonificationAn abstract concept or non-human thing given human qualitiesThe leaves waved in the wind.
Apostrophe (figure of speech)Addressing an inanimate object, abstract concept, or the dead as if they could respond"Death, be not proud."
ConceitAn elaborate, witty comparison between two very different things — deliberately incongruous"The broken heart is like a cracked teapot."
Transferred epithetAn adjective grammatically attached to one noun but logically describing another"an unhappy relationship" — the sadness belongs to the person, not the abstract relationship
Key distinction — simile vs metaphor Both compare two unlike things to help the reader understand the less familiar one through the more familiar one. Simile signals the comparison with "like"/"as"; metaphor states it directly as fact.
Common mistake Calling every comparison a "metaphor" by default. Check for "like" or "as" first — if it's there, it's a simile, not a metaphor.
Exam tip Required phrasing: "The [device] '___' compares X to Y, suggesting that ___." Always name what's being compared to what, not just that a comparison exists.

Sound devices

Sound devices work on the ear before the eye — they build rhythm, mood and mimicry through the physical sound of the words themselves, independent of what those words mean.

DeviceDefinitionExample
AlliterationRepetition of the same consonant sound at the start of two or more nearby wordsKeep the cat from clawing the couch.
AssonanceRepetition of the same vowel sound within nearby wordsShe eats the sweet treats.
ConsonanceRepetition of the same/similar consonant sound within or at the end of nearby wordsThe locket clicked shut.

Contradiction & irony

These devices all rely on a gap — between what's said and meant, between what's expected and what happens, or between two contradictory ideas placed together. Spotting the exact type of gap is what separates the marks.

DeviceDefinitionExample
OxymoronTwo contradictory words placed directly next to each other"deafening silence"
ParadoxA statement that seems contradictory but, on reflection, makes sense"If you do not risk anything, you risk everything."
AntithesisTwo contrasting ideas balanced within the same sentence"one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind"
JuxtapositionTwo images/ideas placed side by side to highlight their contrast"All's fair in love and war."
Verbal ironyWords used to mean the opposite of their literal sense"Brutus is an honourable man" (Antony means the opposite)
Situational ironyA contrast between what's expected to happen and what actually happensA wife sells her hair to buy a watch chain; her husband sells his watch to buy her a hair accessory.
Dramatic ironyThe audience/reader knows something a character doesn'tThe audience knows a villain's plan before the hero does
SarcasmVerbal irony intended to hurt, humiliate or insult"Oh, aren't you just the brightest spark in the bonfire?"
SatireHumour, irony or sarcasm used to ridicule a political/social/human weakness, aiming to provoke changePolitical cartoons; Animal Farm
EpigramA short, witty saying that makes its point concisely"I can resist everything but temptation." — Oscar Wilde
Key distinction — oxymoron vs paradox vs antithesis Oxymoron = two words. Paradox = a whole statement that seems illogical but is true on reflection. Antithesis = two contrasting ideas of roughly equal weight balanced in one sentence, without needing to be illogical.
Key distinction — the three types of irony Ask: is the gap in what someone said (verbal), what happened vs what was expected (situational), or what the audience knows vs what a character knows (dramatic)? Only one answer fits any given example.
Common mistake Writing "this is ironic" without specifying which type. "Irony" alone is not a complete identification in an IEB answer — always name verbal, situational, or dramatic irony specifically.
Exam tip Required phrasing for irony questions: "This is an example of [verbal/situational/dramatic] irony because [what is expected/said] contrasts with [what actually happens/is meant], which suggests ___."

Further literary devices

These devices go beyond the core figures of speech and sound devices. They appear in both language-structures questions and poetry analysis.

DeviceDefinitionExample
AllegoryA story, poem or image in which characters and events symbolise a deeper moral, political or spiritual meaningAnimal Farm is an allegory for the Russian Revolution
AllusionAn unexplained or passing reference to a person, place, event or work of literature"He's no Romeo, but he tries"
AnaphoraRepetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses or lines"I have a dream... I have a dream..."
AnticlimaxWords or ideas arranged in descending order of importance; the writer builds expectations then fails to fulfil them"On the same day, she lost her family, her home and her cell phone"
BathosA sudden, often unintentional shift from the serious to the trivial, creating an anticlimax or comic effectA dramatic love scene that ends with a character tripping over a shoe
Climax (rhetorical)Words, phrases or clauses arranged to build excitement or urgency, saving the most important point for last"I came, I saw, I conquered"
EuphemismA polite or softened way of saying something harsh or unpleasant"He passed away" instead of "he died"; "vertically challenged" instead of "short"
HyperboleExaggeration for strong impact, not meant literally"I've told you the meaning of hyperbole a million times"
InnuendoHints at something without saying it directly, often used for humour or to insinuate something controversial"The most impressive parts of the play were the periods of silence"
LitotesA negative used to express the positive; a form of deliberate understatement"He is no fool" (meaning he is very clever)
MeiosisDeliberate understatement; the opposite of hyperbole"I have a little matter to attend to — I'm getting married in 10 minutes"

SynecdocheA part used to represent a whole, or vice versa"All hands on deck" (hands = sailors)
ParodyAn exaggerated imitation of another work, style, or genre, often for comic effectPride and Prejudice and Zombies
PunA play on words relying on multiple meanings or similar-sounding words"Mine is a long and a sad tale!" said the Mouse. "It is a long tail, certainly," said Alice
Rhetorical questionA question asked to make a point or prompt reflection, not to get an answer"Is this the only future terrorism has to offer?"
Key distinctions to remember Litotes vs meiosis: both understate, but litotes specifically uses a negative ("not bad" = good) while meiosis is any deliberate understatement. Metonymy vs synecdoche: metonymy substitutes a related concept (crown = monarchy); synecdoche substitutes a part for the whole (wheels = car). Anticlimax vs bathos: anticlimax is the structural descent; bathos is the sudden tonal drop that creates it.
Exam tip If you can't remember whether something is metonymy or synecdoche, ask: is the substitute a part of the thing (hands for sailors = synecdoche) or a related concept (the crown for the monarchy = metonymy)?

Common language errors

These are named, testable error types — the exam gives you a flawed sentence and asks you to identify what's wrong with it and why. Knowing the term is half the battle.

ErrorDefinitionExampleThe problem
AmbiguityA word, phrase or sentence can be read with more than one meaning"I gave a bath to my dog wearing a pink t-shirt."Who is wearing the t-shirt — the dog or the speaker?
Tautology / redundancyUsing more words than needed, repeating an idea that adds nothing new"I saw it with my own eyes.""With my own eyes" is redundant — seeing already implies eyes
MalapropismAn incorrect word used in place of one with a similar sound, usually resulting in unintended humour"The mayor welcomed all the guests and extinguished visitors.""Extinguished" should be "distinguished"
Misrelated (dangling) participleA participle unintentionally modifies the wrong noun"Sitting on the bench, the sun slowly sank."Grammatically, this says the sun was sitting on the bench
SpoonerismThe initial sounds of two or more words are swapped, usually for humour"Please sew me to another sheet" (instead of "show me to another seat")Sounds transposed, changing the meaning
Incorrect case of pronounA pronoun's form doesn't match its role, or pronoun use is inconsistent"If one needs anything, you should notify the manager."Switches from "one" to "you" mid-sentence — pick one and stay consistent
Exam tip For "identify the error" questions, read the sentence completely literally first — most of these errors are only visible when you stop assuming the writer's intended meaning and read exactly what's on the page.
Common mistake Confusing a misrelated participle with ambiguity. A misrelated participle specifically involves an opening participial phrase (usually ending in -ing or -ed) attaching to the wrong noun — ambiguity is a broader category that can happen anywhere in a sentence.
Exam tip When correcting a misrelated participle, the fix is almost always to make the subject of the main clause match who's doing the action in the participial phrase: "Sitting on the bench, I watched the sun slowly sink."

Ozymandias

Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792–1822)
I met a traveller from an antique land Who said: "Two vast and trunkless legs of stone Stand in the desert. Near them, on the sand, Half sunk, a shattered visage lies, whose frown, And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command, Tell that its sculptor well those passions read Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things, The hand that mocked them and the heart that fed: And on the pedestal these words appear: 'My name is Ozymandias, king of kings: Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!' Nothing beside remains. Round the decay Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare The lone and level sands stretch far away."

Overview

A traveller describes the ruined statue of an ancient, once-mighty king found in the desert. The sonnet is a story within a story: the speaker relays the traveller's account, who relays what the statue's inscription and shattered face still say. Power, arrogance, and empire all prove temporary — only the desert remains.

Context

Believed inspired by a statue of Ramesses II ("Ozymandias" is his Greek name) that Shelley may have seen or heard about arriving at the British Museum. Written in 1817 as part of a friendly competition with Horace Smith. Shelley was a radical Romantic poet who was expelled from Oxford and spoke out against tyranny — the poem fits his broader political scepticism about absolute power.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormA sonnet (14 lines) — but an irregular one, closer to Petrarchan than Shakespearean
Rhyme schemeABABACDCEDEFEF — unconventional, mirroring the statue's fragmented, broken state
Narrative frameSpeaker → traveller → statue's inscription: each layer of retelling distances Ozymandias further from any real power
TurnThe proud inscription ("Look on my Works…and despair!") is immediately undercut by "Nothing beside remains" — dramatic irony built into the structure itself

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Metaphor"colossal Wreck"The statue's remains become a symbol for the total collapse of Ozymandias's power
Irony (situational)"Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!" / "Nothing beside remains"The king demanded awe at his achievements — instead there's nothing left to be awed by
Personification"the hand that mocked them"Suggests the sculptor may have subtly mocked the tyrant even while carving him
Imagery of emptiness"boundless and bare," "lone and level sands"The desert's vast, featureless scale dwarfs any human claim to permanence

Themes

The impermanence of power and human achievement · the destructive force of time · pride and arrogance · art's power to outlast politics · the insignificance of humanity against nature and time.

Message to remember Political power fades to nothing; art (the sculptor's work, and Shelley's poem itself) is what actually survives. Ozymandias's arrogance is precisely what the poem preserves and mocks.

Imagery categories

CategoryExamplesSuggests
Desert imagery"desert," "sand," "boundless and bare," "lone and level sands"Emptiness, isolation, the erasure of human achievement by time and nature
Fragmented statue imagery"Two vast and trunkless legs of stone"Only fragments survive — Ozymandias's power has literally been reduced to pieces
Facial imagery"a shattered visage," "frown," "wrinkled lip," "sneer"The statue preserves the ruler's arrogant personality even though his empire is gone
Monumental imagery"pedestal," "colossal Wreck"Signals the scale of the wealth and power that has since collapsed into ruin

Worked example

Q: Comment on the effectiveness of the juxtaposition in the poem's final lines. (3) Model answer

The juxtaposition between Ozymandias's boastful inscription — "Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!" — and the immediately following "Nothing beside remains" is highly effective. Placing the ruler's arrogant command directly beside the emptiness that surrounds it forces the reader to measure his claim against reality in a single breath, exposing the gap between what he believed about his own permanence and what time has actually done to his empire.

Background — Shelley the radical Shelley was expelled from Oxford for writing on atheism and was known for his outspoken opposition to tyranny and inherited power. Read against this, Ozymandias isn't just a meditation on time — it's a pointed political statement: absolute rulers who believe themselves untouchable are always, eventually, proven wrong.
Exam tip If asked about the sonnet form, the required point is that Shelley subverts a form traditionally used for love poetry to instead critique political power — the choice of form is itself ironic.

Mirror

Sylvia Plath
I am silver and exact. I have no preconceptions. Whatever I see I swallow immediately Just as it is, unmisted by love or dislike. I am not cruel, only truthful – The eye of a little god, four-cornered. Most of the time I meditate on the opposite wall. It is pink, with speckles. I have looked at it so long I think it is part of my heart. But it flickers. Faces and darkness separate us over and over. Now I am a lake. A woman bends over me, Searching my reaches for what she really is. Then she turns to those liars, the candles or the moon. I see her back, and reflect it faithfully. She rewards me with tears and an agitation of hands. I am important to her. She comes and goes. Each morning it is her face that replaces the darkness. In me she has drowned a young girl, and in me an old woman Rises toward her day after day, like a terrible fish.

Overview

The mirror itself narrates the poem in first person, insisting it shows only unbiased truth. In the second stanza it becomes a lake, watched by a woman who searches its surface for her real self and finds, instead, the slow arrival of old age.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormTwo nine-line stanzas, free verse, no regular rhyme — the lack of rhyme reinforces the mirror's claim to be unpatterned, exact, and free of human bias
VoiceFirst-person personification — the mirror speaks, giving it authority and detachment at once
ShiftStanza 1 = mirror on the wall (objective, static). Stanza 2 = mirror as lake (searched, personal, mysterious) — the shift deepens the metaphor from surface reflection to psychological depth
Climax"In me she has drowned a young girl, and in me an old woman / Rises" — youth submerged, age emerging, closing on "a terrible fish"

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Personification"I am silver and exact," "I meditate"Gives the mirror a voice and a claim to objectivity the reader must weigh against the woman's suffering
Metaphor"Now I am a lake"Deepens the reflection from a flat, literal surface to something the woman must search within
Simile"like a terrible fish"Old age is rendered as something grotesque, alien, and inescapably rising toward her
Diction — "liars""those liars, the candles or the moon"Soft, flattering light is condemned as dishonest compared to the mirror's harsh accuracy

Key contrasts

ContrastMeaning
Truth vs illusionThe mirror's honesty vs the flattering "liars" of candlelight/moonlight
Objectivity vs emotionThe mirror is "unmisted by love or dislike" while the woman responds with "tears"
Youth vs old age"a young girl" drowned, "an old woman" rising

Diction highlights

Word/phraseEffect
"swallow immediately"Suggests the mirror absorbs everything instantly and without judgement, reinforcing total objectivity
"unmisted"Implies emotion is a kind of fog that clouds perception — the mirror claims to see past it
"little god"Gives the mirror an authority and power to judge that borders on the divine
"rewards me with tears"Bitterly ironic — the woman's emotional reaction is framed as a "reward," when it's clearly pain
"drowned" / "Rises"Violent, opposing verbs that stage the loss of youth and the arrival of old age as a kind of death and resurrection

Worked example

Q: Why might the candles and moon be described as "liars" (line 12)? (2) Model answer

Candlelight and moonlight are called "liars" because their soft, flattering glow disguises the true signs of ageing that the mirror shows without mercy. The word exposes the woman's preference for comforting illusion over the mirror's harsh accuracy — and by extension, the human tendency to seek out flattering surroundings rather than face uncomfortable truths.

Message to remember Truth is neutral — it's the viewer's fear of ageing that turns a factual reflection into something painful. The poem questions whether identity can survive the passage of time, and whether we can ever really face our own reflection honestly.
Exam tip If asked to evaluate the "lake" metaphor's effectiveness, the strong answer links it to depth: a lake doesn't just reflect surface appearance the way a flat mirror does — it suggests something the woman is searching for beneath the surface, i.e. her true identity.

Crossing Over

Chris Mann
I drew abreast of them in the drizzle that drifted across a railway-bridge, two young adults, wrapped in scarves, who'd spoken that evening in church. They'd stood in front of the altar, a man and a woman, beside a priest and great bowls of aloes and lilies and told us how they lived with Aids. The streets they climbed, so slowly that Sunday night had gone all quiet, as if the fear of some terrible event had emptied the whole small town. The feeble blurs of the street-lights, sunk in the mist above the bridge, had greyed the shapes in front of me to phantoms in some life beyond. A moment! I said to them, hollowly. Thank you for visiting our church! They paused, and turning their eyes, their wan and haggard young faces towards me in the drizzled gloom greeted me with a cheery warmth that made me want to curse aloud and turn my face aside and weep. They drew back, sensing my distress. I hugged them, and for a moment, a strange short eternity out of time I felt their presences hug me back, I who lived this side of the tracks, in the suburbs, the gardens of life, And they who'd already crossed over into the hard streets of life-in-death.

Overview

The speaker crosses a railway bridge in the mist and recognises a young couple living with HIV/AIDS who had spoken earlier at church. What begins as polite distance turns into a genuine, physical moment of human connection — an embrace that briefly closes the gap between the speaker's protected life and their reality.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormFour eight-line stanzas — the regular structure contrasts with the speaker's emotional disturbance
Narrative shapeObservation (st. 1) → memory of church (st. 1) → mounting unease (st. 2) → sudden emotional reaction and connection (st. 3–4)
EnjambmentSentences run across line breaks throughout, creating a flowing, unsettled movement that mirrors the speaker's shifting thoughts
Final line"the hard streets of life-in-death" — a hyphenated, deliberately shocking phrase closing the poem on the couple's reality

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Setting/atmosphere"drizzle," "feeble blurs of the street-lights," "mist"A cold, uncertain, half-visible world reflecting the fragility of the couple's situation
Juxtaposition"I who lived this side of the tracks, in the suburbs, the gardens of life" vs "they who'd already crossed over"Stark contrast between the speaker's safety and the couple's proximity to death
Oxymoron/paradox"life-in-death"Captures the couple's suspended state — alive, but marked by terminal illness
Minimal punctuation / line breaks"I hugged them, and for a moment, / a strange short eternity out of time"Creates a breathless rhythm mirroring the intensity of the embrace

The title's double meaning

"Crossing Over" refers literally to the couple crossing the railway bridge, but also to their having "crossed over" into a life shaped by serious illness and social isolation — and to the speaker's own emotional crossing, from distance and discomfort into empathy.

Tone

ToneEvidence
Uneasy / detached (opening)The speaker tries to maintain polite distance: "A moment! I said to them, hollowly."
CompassionateHe eventually "hugged them" — physical contact signals genuine empathy
Warm and tenderThe couple greet him with "a cheery warmth" despite their illness
ReflectiveThe speaker recognises the social distance between himself and the couple: "I who lived this side of the tracks"
Ambiguous / unsettling (ending)"life-in-death" leaves the reader without a comfortable resolution

Purpose

To explore society's fear of serious illness and death; to challenge social separation and prejudice; to show the importance of empathy and human contact; to reveal the emotional complexity of encountering suffering; to question the boundaries between "us" and "them."

Worked example

Q: Explain how the setting enhances the poem's mood. (3) Model answer

The setting — drizzle drifting across a railway bridge, street-lights reduced to "feeble blurs" sunk in mist — creates a cold, uncertain, half-visible atmosphere. This mirrors the precariousness of the couple's situation: just as the mist obscures clear sight, their illness places them in an uncertain space between the visible, healthy world and one shaped by mortality, heightening the reader's sense of fragility and compassion.

Message to remember Compassion can break down the social distance illness creates. The poem doesn't resolve or minimise the couple's suffering — it ends on the harsh reality of "life-in-death" — but insists that human contact still matters within that reality.
Exam tip On "why doesn't the poet name the couple" — the required point is universalising: leaving them unnamed keeps the focus on their shared condition and experience rather than individual biography, making the poem's message apply more broadly.

For Oom Piet

Finuala Dowling
When I was least happy in life my daughter led me across the road to a neighbour whom I did not know with the purpose of a toddler who does not see gates and fences but the ducks and puppies on the other side. You spoke to me in the same low murmuring tones you used for lame horses and bilious dogs. Crops, flowers and poultry were our theme: you made me tea and wiped the counter, you said 'Foeitog' and 'I don't hope so.' You guessed. You told me I was beautiful, In the courtly way of an old man. One day when it rained you phoned from your side and said 'I'm sending some ducks for Beatrix' ̶ you always called her 'Beatrix' – out the window we saw them, waddling over, in the way of ducks when directed by Oom Piet. You moved and I moved, but one thing stays: the sense of a rescue, somehow being saved.

Overview

A personal tribute to a neighbour, Oom Piet, who offers the speaker gentle, unromantic kindness during a period of unhappiness. Her daughter's childlike lack of social barriers leads them to him; his quiet warmth — tea, small compliments, ordinary rural conversation — becomes a lasting source of comfort, described at the end as a "rescue."

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormFive uneven, free-verse stanzas with no regular rhyme — reads like natural, conversational memory or storytelling
ShapeMoves from unhappiness (st. 1) → the daughter's innocent lead → Oom Piet's kindness (st. 2–3) → a specific memory of the ducks (st. 4) → reflective conclusion (st. 5)
Closing paradox"You moved and I moved, but one thing stays" — physical/circumstantial change contrasted with something emotionally permanent

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Metaphor"who does not see gates and fences"The daughter's innocence removes the social barriers adults construct between strangers
Simile of tone"the same low murmuring tones / you used for lame horses and bilious dogs"Oom Piet's gentleness toward the speaker is equated with his gentleness toward vulnerable animals — care without condescension
Diction"courtly way of an old man"His compliment is framed as old-fashioned respect, not romantic or sexual interest
Paradox"You moved and I moved, but one thing stays"Even though circumstances and distance changed, the emotional impact of the encounter remains fixed

Themes

Kindness from unexpected sources · human connection unrestricted by age or social distance · the endurance of platonic friendship · the quiet nature of real comfort · loneliness and its remedy.

Tone (expanded)

ToneEvidence
Vulnerable"When I was least happy in life" opens the poem in open emotional honesty
Gentle"low murmuring tones" creates a soft, unthreatening atmosphere
Affectionate / nostalgicThe speaker recalls tea, conversation, and the ducks with clear warmth
HumorousThe image of ducks "waddling over… when directed by Oom Piet" adds gentle comedy
Respectful"courtly way of an old man" presents Oom Piet with dignity, not condescension
Grateful / hopefulThe poem moves from unhappiness to "the sense of a rescue, somehow being saved"

Purpose

To celebrate the power of ordinary kindness; to show that human connection can provide emotional rescue; to show how small, undramatic actions can have lasting impact; to contrast social barriers with genuine human connection; to suggest that children may perceive connection more naturally than adults; to show that compassion doesn't always require words.

Worked example

Q: Identify and comment on FOUR characteristics of Oom Piet shown in the poem. (4) Model answer

Gentle and caring — he speaks in "the same low murmuring tones" used for injured animals. Attentive and perceptive — "You guessed" shows he read her unhappiness without being told. Courteous and respectful — his compliment is given "in the courtly way of an old man," free of romantic intent. Generous and thoughtful — small practical gestures like making her tea and sending ducks for her daughter show sustained, quiet care rather than a single grand gesture.

Message to remember Consolation doesn't have to be dramatic. Ordinary, undemanding kindness — tea, small talk, a compliment given respectfully — can function as genuine rescue for someone who is struggling.
Exam tip When asked to comment on tone, always support with the specific diction: "gentle," "affectionate," and "nostalgic" tones are best evidenced through "low murmuring tones," "courtly way," and the domestic details (tea, ducks, poultry).

Constantly Risking Absurdity

Lawrence Ferlinghetti
Constantly risking absurdity and death whenever he performs above the heads of his audience the poet like an acrobat climbs on rime to a high wire of his own making and balancing on eyebeams above a sea of faces paces his way to the other side of day performing entrechats and sleight-of-foot tricks and other high theatrics and all without mistaking anything for what it may not be For he's the super realist who must perforce perceive taut truth before the taking of each stance or step in his supposed advance toward that still higher perch where Beauty stands and waits with gravity to start her death-defying leap And he a little charleychaplin man who may or may not catch her fair eternal form spreadeagled in the empty air of existence.

Overview

An extended metaphor comparing the poet to a circus acrobat, risking humiliation ("absurdity") and creative failure ("death") every time he performs. The poet must balance precision and creativity while attempting to reach Beauty — something elusive, almost supernatural — with no guarantee of success.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormFree verse, no fixed stanza structure or rhyme scheme — reflects the freedom and unpredictability of the creative process

Stealing

Carol Ann Duffy
The most unusual thing I ever stole? A snowman. Midnight. He looked magnificent; a tall, white mute beneath the winter moon. I wanted him, a mate with a mind as cold as the slice of ice within my own brain. I started with the head. Better off dead than giving in, not taking what you want. He weighed a ton; his torso, frozen stiff, hugged to my chest, a fierce chill piercing my gut. Part of the thrill was knowing that children would cry in the morning. Life's tough. Sometimes I steal things I don't need. I joy-ride cars to nowhere, break into houses just to have a look. I'm a mucky ghost, leave a mess, maybe pinch a camera. I watch my gloved hand twisting the doorknob. A stranger's bedroom. Mirrors. I sigh like this — Aah. It took some time. Reassembled in the yard, he didn't look the same. I took a run and booted him. Again. Again. My breath ripped out in rags. It seems daft now. Then I was standing alone amongst lumps of snow, sick of the world. Boredom. Mostly I'm so bored I could eat myself. One time, I stole a guitar and thought I might learn to play. I nicked a bust of Shakespeare once, flogged it, but the snowman was the strangest. You don't understand a word I'm saying, do you?

Overview

A first-person confessional monologue from a thief who stole a snowman — not for its value, but out of boredom and a need for control and companionship. The poem widens into a broader confession of compulsive stealing and destructive behaviour, ending with the speaker directly challenging the reader's understanding of him.

Context

Duffy has said the poem was inspired by the real theft of a neighbour's children's snowman, and described it as "a little political" — written against the backdrop of 1980s Britain under Margaret Thatcher, a period of unemployment, social inequality and youth alienation.

Structure

ShapeMoves from the specific event (the snowman) to a general confession of other thefts, ending on the isolating direct address: "You don't understand a word I'm saying, do you?"
Fragmented syntaxShort, disjointed sentences ("Boredom." / "Mirrors.") mirror the speaker's unstable, restless state of mind

DeviceQuoteEffect

Themes

Loneliness and alienation · boredom as a destructive force · the need for control and connection · society's failure to understand outsiders · destructive/anti-social behaviour as a symptom of emotional emptiness.

Message to remember The poem uses a strange, almost comic crime to expose something serious: profound loneliness and boredom driving destructive behaviour. The final direct address leaves the reader implicated — unable to fully understand or connect with the speaker, mirroring his own isolation.

Each of us is a passenger seated in one huge compartment going we do not know where all strangers thrown together by chance who travel without arriving; Who can read the whispers of your mind when they are hidden even from you? Overview

An extended metaphor of life as a train journey, in which all people are fellow passengers — physically close, sharing the same compartment, yet never able to fully know one another or even themselves. Even the closest relationships retain one private, inaccessible part.

Context — the Johari Window

The poem connects to the Johari Window, a concept for understanding what is known/unknown to oneself and others. The poem argues that some part of the self remains unknown even to the person themselves — closing off any possibility of total understanding, no matter how much effort is made.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormFree verse, 25 lines, no regular rhyme or metre — reflects the natural, unpredictable movement of human thought and relationships
MovementFrom the individual ("Each of us") to the shared human journey ("one huge compartment") and back to the private individual — physical closeness giving way to emotional/mental distance
Repetition"one part... one small part... one part" emphasises that the inaccessible core of a person, though small, is permanent and significant
Isolated final line"Forever strangers." stands alone, delivering the poem's conclusion with maximum weight

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Extended metaphorlife as a train journey ("passenger," "compartment," "travel without arriving")Suggests humans share existence but not necessarily true connection or a known destination
Imagery of confinement"one huge / compartment"Physical closeness within a confined shared space, despite emotional separateness
Metaphor"whispers of your mind"Presents thought as quiet, private, and difficult for others — even the self — to fully access
Metaphor"open a window in the chambers of your heart"Represents the effort of emotional communication and vulnerability

Themes

Human isolation and alienation · the limits of communication and understanding · the mystery of individual identity · relationships formed by chance · self-knowledge (or the lack of it).

Message to remember However hard people try to connect and understand each other, a small, permanent, unreachable part of every individual remains private — including from themselves. Genuine effort at connection is still worthwhile, but complete understanding is impossible.
Exam tip On the significance of the title, the required point: the word "forever" makes the separation permanent, not merely circumstantial — even sustained effort and intimacy cannot fully close the gap between people.

The Sun Rising

John Donne
Busy old fool, unruly Sun, Why dost thou thus, Through windows, and through curtains, call on us? Must to thy motions lovers' seasons run? Saucy pedantic wretch, go chide Late school-boys and sour 'prentices, Go tell court-huntsmen that the King will ride, Call country ants to harvest offices; Love, all alike, no season knows, nor clime, Nor hours, days, months, which are the rags of time. Ask for those kings whom thou saw'st yesterday, And thou shalt hear, 'All here in one bed lay.' She's all States, and all Princes I; Nothing else is. Princes do but play us; compared to this, All honour's mimic; all wealth alchemy. Thou, Sun, art half as happy as we, In that the world's contracted thus; Thine age asks ease, and since thy duties be To warm the world, that's done in warming us. Overview

A lover berates the rising sun for intruding on his and his beloved's time together, arguing that love exists outside ordinary time and worldly concerns. Through escalating hyperbole, the speaker declares the lovers' bed the centre of the entire universe.

Context — an aubade & metaphysical poetry

The poem is an aubade — a poem about lovers forced to part at dawn. Donne was a Metaphysical poet, a group concerned with profound questions of existence and meaning, whose poems often take the form of witty, logical arguments fused with intense emotion.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormThree ten-line stanzas, written as a dramatic monologue directly addressing the sun
Stanza 1Challenges the sun's authority; ends by declaring love "no season knows, nor clime"
Stanza 2Claims the speaker could block out the sun's power entirely; the world's wealth is imagined as contained "in one bed"
Stanza 3The lovers become literally "all States, and all Princes" — the bedroom becomes the sun's "center" and "sphere"
Rhetorical modeFrequent questions and commands ("Why dost thou thus?," "Look") create a lively, argumentative rhythm typical of metaphysical poetry

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Personification"Busy old fool, unruly Sun"Reduces the sun to a meddlesome, disrespected intruder rather than a cosmic power
Hyperbole"She's all States, and all Princes I"Elevates the lovers to encompass all worldly power and wealth
Paradox"This bed thy center is, these walls thy sphere"A small private room becomes the entire universe — the sun now orbits them
Metaphor"hours, days, months, which are the rags of time"Ordinary time is dismissed as worthless scraps compared to timeless love

Themes

The power of love · love versus time · love versus worldly power and wealth · the private world of lovers as a complete universe.

Message to remember Love is presented as more powerful, more valuable, and more permanent than any worldly authority — political, financial, or even cosmic. The escalating hyperbole isn't meant literally; it dramatises just how completely love can reorganise a person's sense of what matters.
Exam tip On tone questions, track the shift: confrontational and mocking toward the sun in stanza 1, confident and triumphant by stanza 3 — the tone escalates alongside the hyperbole.

Remember

Christina Rossetti
Remember me when I am gone away, Gone far away into the silent land; When you can no more hold me by the hand, Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay. Remember me when no more day by day You tell me of our future that you plann'd: Only remember me; you understand It will be late to counsel then or pray. Yet if you should forget me for a while And afterwards remember, do not grieve: For if the darkness and corruption leave A vestige of the thoughts that once I had, Better by far you should forget and smile Than that you should remember and be sad.

Overview

A speaker facing death asks her beloved to remember her — but the poem's emotional centre shifts in the final six lines, as she decides the beloved's happiness matters more than being remembered at all.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormA Petrarchan sonnet: octave (8 lines) + sestet (6 lines), rhyme scheme abba abba cde cde
OctaveRepeated imperative "Remember me" (lines 1, 5) creates insistence and emotional urgency around the speaker's coming death
Volta"Yet if you should forget me for a while" (line 9) — the word "Yet" signals the turn from wanting to be remembered to accepting being forgotten
SestetBecomes generous and selfless, concluding "Better by far you should forget and smile / Than that you should remember and be sad"

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Metaphor / euphemism"gone far away into the silent land"Death is softened into a journey to a quiet place, avoiding the word "death" directly
Repetition"Remember me" (lines 1, 5)Builds emotional urgency and insistence in the octave
Contrast (octave vs sestet)"Remember me" ↔ "forget and smile"Tracks the speaker's emotional development from a desire to be remembered to selfless acceptance

Themes

Death and separation · memory and forgetting · love and grief · selflessness · acceptance · prioritising the happiness of the living over one's own legacy.

Message to remember Written by Rossetti at just 19, the poem moves from a natural, human desire to be remembered after death toward something more mature: real love means caring more about the other person's wellbeing than about your own memory. Grief shouldn't outweigh the living person's right to move forward and be happy.
Exam tip When asked to identify the volta, always name both the word that signals it ("Yet," line 9) and the shift in thinking it introduces — identifying the word alone is only half the answer.

Superstition

Minji Karibo
I know that when a grumbling old woman Is the first thing I meet in the morning I must rush back to bed And cover my head. That wandering sheep on a sultry afternoon Are really men come from their dark graves To walk in light In mortal sight. That when my left hand or eyelid twitches Or when an owl hoots from a nearby tree I should need pluck It means bad luck That drink spilled goes to ancestral spirits, That witches dance in clumps of bananas; That crumbs must be left in pots and plates Until the morn For babes unborn. That it's wrong to stand in doorways at dusk For the ghosts must pass—they have right of way! That when a hidden root trips me over Fault's not in my foot. It's an evil root. That if I sleep with feet towards the door I'll not long be fit I know it—Yes I know it!

Overview

A first-person catalogue of superstitious beliefs, listed one after another with total conviction — from meeting a "grumbling old woman" to sleeping with feet toward the door. The poem uses the speaker's own certainty to explore how ordinary events get transformed into supernatural warnings.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormA single continuous list, structured around the repeated word "That," each introducing a new superstition
FramingOpens and closes with "I know" — insisting on the speaker's certainty at both ends of the poem
Effect of the list structureThe cumulative, repetitive listing mimics how superstition operates — through accumulated, unquestioned habit rather than logical examination

Key imagery & devices

CategoryExamplesEffect
Imagery of darkness and light"dark graves," "walk in light"Marks the boundary between the world of the dead and the living
Imagery of bodily signs"my left hand or eyelid twitches"An ordinary physical sensation is read as a supernatural warning
Metaphor"men come from their dark graves / To walk in light"Sheep are reimagined as the dead returning to the living world
Personification"witches dance in clumps of bananas," "the ghosts must pass—they have right of way!"Gives ghosts and supernatural forces human agency and rights within ordinary spaces
Hyperbole"I must rush back to bed / And cover my head"An exaggerated reaction to something ordinary reveals the real power superstition holds over behaviour

Central contrast

The poem consistently sets ordinary reality against supernatural belief: natural vs supernatural, human reason vs superstition, rational explanation vs imagined danger. The recurring pattern is that superstition rejects rational explanations ("Fault's not in my foot") in favour of supernatural ones ("It's an evil root").

Themes

The power of superstition over behaviour · fear of the unknown · the boundary between the living and the dead · how ordinary events acquire supernatural meaning · cultural belief systems.

Message to remember Superstition gives supernatural meaning to ordinary, unexplained events, letting people feel they understand and control an uncertain world — even when that "understanding" restricts their freedom and behaviour.
Exam tip On tone or diction questions, note that the diction is simple and conversational ("I know," "I must rush back to bed") — this makes the poem sound like someone matter-of-factly explaining familiar, unquestioned beliefs rather than arguing a case.

The Poet

William Shakespeare — from A Midsummer Night's Dream
The lunatic, the lover and the poet Are of imagination all compact:– One sees more devils than vast hell can hold, That is, the madman: the lover, all as frantic, Sees Helen's beauty in a brow of Egypt: The poet's eye, in fine frenzy rolling, Doth glance from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven; And, as imagination bodies forth The forms of things unknown, the poet's pen Turns them to shapes, and gives to airy nothing A local habitation and a name.

Overview

Spoken by Theseus in Act 5 of A Midsummer Night's Dream, this speech groups the lunatic, the lover, and the poet together as figures whose powerful imaginations let them see the world differently from ordinary people. The poet is presented as the most powerful of the three, because he alone can give invisible ideas physical form and name — turning "airy nothing" into something real.

Structure

FeatureDetail
FormA single continuous 11-line passage — compact and concentrated
Escalating attentionThe lunatic gets a brief description (lines 3–4), the lover slightly more (lines 4–5), the poet by far the most detailed (lines 6–11) — suggesting the poet is the most complex figure of the three
ProgressionPerception ("sees") → imagination ("bodies forth") → creation ("turns them to shapes") → naming ("a local habitation and a name") — moving from passive seeing to active creation

Key imagery & devices

DeviceQuoteEffect
Allusion"Sees Helen's beauty in a brow of Egypt"Invokes Helen of Troy as the symbol of legendary, idealised beauty, showing how the lover's imagination transforms the ordinary into the extraordinary
Paradox"airy nothing" given "A local habitation and a name"Something explicitly called "nothing" is nevertheless made real and permanent through poetry
Personification"imagination bodies forth," "the poet's pen / Turns them to shapes"Gives imagination and the pen an active, almost creative human agency
Repetition"from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven"The reversed repetition suggests the boundless, unrestricted scope of the poet's vision

Themes

The power of imagination · the poet as creator · the relationship between madness, love, and creativity · language's power to give form to the unknown.

Message to remember Grouping the poet with the lunatic and the lover is deliberately provocative — it suggests creative imagination can resemble madness, but also elevates the poet above the other two: only the poet can communicate his imagination to others by giving it "a name," making it shareable and permanent.
Exam tip On the effect of "from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven," the required point: the reversed repetition creates rhythmic balance while emphasising that the poet's imagination is unrestricted by any single fixed direction or boundary.

Unseen poetry strategy

The unseen poem is designed to test whether you can apply the same analytical skills you used on the 16 seen poems to something you have never read before. There is nothing to memorise — only a method to practise.

The reading sequence

StepActionWhy
1Read the questions firstTells you what to hunt for during the first read
2Read the poem once for overall meaning — what literally happens or is being saidBuilds context so close-reading is never done blind
3Read the poem again, this time annotating: underline key images, circle repeated words, note tone shiftsForces active engagement rather than passive re-reading
4Answer each question using the identify → quote → explain effect formulaSame formula as every other Paper 1 question

What to look for on any unseen poem

ElementAsk
SpeakerWho is speaking? To whom? What is their attitude?
SubjectWhat is the poem literally about? What is it really about (deeper meaning)?
StructureHow many stanzas? Regular or irregular? Where does the tone or argument shift?
ImageryWhat are the 2–3 most striking images? What senses do they appeal to?
SoundAny rhyme, rhythm, alliteration, sibilance, onomatopoeia? What effect does the sound create?
ToneWhat is the speaker's attitude? Does it shift? Where? What diction signals the shift?
MessageWhat is the poem ultimately saying about its subject?
Exam tip The unseen poem question is not harder than the seen poem question — it just can't be pre-studied. The same devices, the same answer formula, and the same marking criteria apply. If you can analyse a seen poem well, you can analyse an unseen poem well.

Love and trust

Love in Othello is presented as both the characters' greatest strength and their greatest vulnerability. Desdemona's love is unconditional — she defends her marriage, forgives Othello even as he kills her. Othello's love is intense but fragile — it collapses the moment trust is undermined.

Gender and power

Women in Othello are controlled, silenced, and ultimately killed by the men around them. Desdemona is smothered; Emilia is stabbed; Bianca is publicly accused. The play's most direct critique of this comes from Emilia.

Deception & malice

Not every character who deceives in Othello is malicious. The play distinguishes sharply between calculated, strategic deception (Iago) and defensive, emotional deception (everyone else). Understanding this distinction is essential for any essay on the play.

Iago — calculated and malicious deception

ActDeceptive actMethod
Act 1Presents himself as loyal to Othello while secretly hating him"I am not what I am" — his entire public persona is a performance
Act 2Engineers Cassio's disgrace by getting him drunk and provoking a fightThen poses as Cassio's friend and advises him to seek Desdemona's help — creating the appearance of an affair
Act 3Plants suspicion through insinuation, not accusation; fabricates handkerchief evidence"Ha! I like not that" — he never directly accuses Desdemona, forcing Othello to draw the conclusions himself
Act 4Stages the eavesdropping scene so Othello sees Cassio laughing about Bianca but believes it's about DesdemonaPushes Othello toward murder: "Strangle her in her bed, even the bed she hath contaminated"
Act 5Kills Roderigo to prevent exposure; kills Emilia when she reveals the truthFinal act of control: "Demand me nothing. What you know, you know. From this time forth I never will speak word"
Why Iago's deception is uniquely dangerous He doesn't lie outright — he insinuates, suggests, and lets others fill the gaps. His technique is to plant a seed ("Ha! I like not that") then withdraw, forcing the victim to construct the lie themselves. This makes the jealousy feel self-generated rather than imposed, which is why Othello believes it so completely.

The intersection that creates tragedy

Shakespeare suggests that tragedy doesn't arise from evil alone, but from the collision between calculated villainy and ordinary human weakness. Desdemona's protective secrecy about the handkerchief, Emilia's naïve loyalty, Othello's insecurity — none of these are malicious, but each one is exploited by Iago to devastating effect. The innocent deceptions become the raw material for the malicious one.

Exam tip If asked to compare deception in the play, structure your essay around this distinction: Iago's deception is strategic, sustained, and designed to harm; the other characters' deceptions are defensive, emotional, and unaware of their consequences. The tragedy lies in how the second type feeds the first.
Common mistake Treating all deception in the play as equally blameworthy. Desdemona hiding the handkerchief loss is not morally equivalent to Iago fabricating evidence of adultery — the play demands you distinguish between intent, method, and consequence.

Model introduction paragraph

Q: Discuss how Shakespeare presents jealousy as a destructive force in Othello. Model introduction

In Shakespeare's tragedy Othello, jealousy operates not as a natural emotion but as a weapon deliberately manufactured and deployed by Iago to destroy everyone around him. While Iago himself identifies jealousy as "the green-eyed monster which doth mock the meat it feeds on," it is his calculated manipulation — through insinuation, fabricated evidence, and strategic timing — that transforms Othello from an eloquent, respected general into a man who murders his innocent wife. This essay will argue that Shakespeare presents jealousy as destructive precisely because it is self-consuming: once planted, it feeds on the victim's own insecurities and requires almost no further evidence to grow, as shown through Othello's psychological collapse in Acts 3 and 4, Iago's exploitation of the handkerchief in Act 3, and the catastrophic consequences in Act 5.

Common mistake Retelling the plot act by act instead of building a thematic argument. Every paragraph should open with an analytical point (not an event), use a quote to support it, then explain how that quote proves your thesis. "In Act 3, Iago tells Othello..." is narration. "Shakespeare uses Iago's insinuation technique in Act 3 to demonstrate that jealousy requires almost no evidence to take hold" is analysis.
Exam tip Draw evidence from at least three different acts to show you know the whole play. An essay that only references Acts 3–5 suggests you only studied the jealousy/murder sections.
Exam tip When discussing Othello as a tragic hero, always address the tension between his outsider status (which makes him vulnerable) and his own choices (which make him culpable). A one-sided "Othello is purely a victim of Iago" answer ignores his agency; a one-sided "Othello is to blame" answer ignores the systemic racism and manipulation he faces.
Transactional Writing

Overview & approach

Transactional writing tests your ability to write for a specific audience, in a specific format, with a specific purpose — and to sound like you mean it.

What the question gives you

ElementWhat to look for
StimulusA quote, image, headline, advertisement, passage, or scenario that sets the direction
FormatThe specific text type you must write (speech, editorial, letter, blog, etc.)
AudienceWho you are writing for — determines register, tone, and word choice
PurposeWhat you are trying to achieve — inform, persuade, entertain, argue, reflect

The SHEEP method for generating content

When you need to build an argument or generate points quickly, run the topic through these five lenses and pick the strongest three.

LetterLensAsk
SSocialWhat impact does this have on people and communities?
HHealthIs it causing harm to the human body or mind?
EEconomicIs someone profiting or losing out financially?
EEnvironmentalDoes it create waste, pollution, or damage ecosystems?
PPoliticalWhat is the government doing or failing to do about it?

Voice — what the examiner is looking for

Voice is not just stylistic flair. According to the examiner, it is "a vehicle for writing that is fluid and consistent, strengthened by conviction and structural strategy. It must be original, fresh and sparkly." In practice, this means: believe in what you are saying (or convincingly pretend to), sustain the same register and tone throughout, and use language inventively rather than generically.

Audience + purpose = register Your audience determines what you write and how you write it. Your purpose determines why you are writing. Together, they set the register. A blog for teenagers about climate change demands a different voice from a formal letter to a principal about the same topic — even though the content overlaps.
Exam tip Read the question twice before you start: once for the topic, once for the format and audience. Students who lose marks on transactional writing almost always do so because they wrote a good piece in the wrong format or the wrong register, not because their ideas were weak.
Common mistake Writing a generic essay with a format heading stuck on top. A speech must sound like a speech (direct address, rhetorical devices, rapport-building); a blog must sound like a blog (conversational, personal, punchy). The format is not decoration — it shapes the whole piece.

Tone word bank

Tone comes from either the head (intellectual) or the heart (emotional). Choosing the right tone word in your planning shapes everything you write.

Head (intellectual)Heart (emotional)

There is no such thing as an "informative tone" "Informative" describes a purpose, not a tone. Tone is the attitude behind the information. A factual report and a sarcastic editorial can both inform the reader — but their tones (detached vs mocking) are completely different. Always name the specific attitude, not the function.

Rhetorical devices for persuasive writing

These are the tools that turn a competent piece of transactional writing into a compelling one. Use them deliberately and sparingly — three well-placed devices are worth more than ten scattered ones.

The persuasive appeals

AppealDefinitionExample
Ethos (credibility)Convincing through the authority or trustworthiness of the speakerCiting an expert; referencing your own experience; using a respected figure's endorsement
Pathos (emotion)Convincing by creating an emotional response — sympathy, anger, fear, hopeA personal anecdote about a child affected by the issue; vivid sensory description of suffering
Logos (logic)Convincing through facts, statistics, and logical reasoning"73% of respondents reported..."; cause-and-effect chains; cost-benefit analysis
Exam tip A strong persuasive piece uses all three appeals: ethos to establish why you should be listened to, logos to prove your case, and pathos to make the audience care. Leading with pathos and closing with logos (or vice versa) is a reliable structure.

Sensationalism (for analysis)

Sensationalism occurs when news stories are selected and worded to excite rather than inform. Tactics include focusing on "otherness," describing crime in shocking detail, using emotive headlines disproportionate to the actual event, and intentionally omitting context. Being able to identify sensationalism is a visual-literacy and comprehension skill.

Common mistake Listing devices without using them. Writing "I will now use a rhetorical question" in your speech is not using one — it's narrating one. The device should be invisible to the reader and felt, not announced.
