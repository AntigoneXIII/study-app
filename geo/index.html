Drainage Basins & Drainage Patterns

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

Core terminology

  • Drainage basin — the area containing a river and all of its tributaries.
  • Watershed — the high-lying land (boundary) separating one drainage basin/tributary from another.
  • Catchment area — the area of land that naturally collects water into a drainage basin.
  • Interfluve — high land separating one tributary valley from another.
  • Confluence — the point where two or more rivers meet.
  • Thalweg — the line connecting the lowest/deepest points along a river channel; shows the line of fastest flow.
Exam patternThese definitions are consistently asked as 1–2 mark recall questions across your papers (2024 May, 2024 Trials, 2023 Trials). Know them word-perfect.

Drainage patterns know all 8

Stream Order & River Discharge

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

Stream order

A ranking system used to describe and compare drainage basins.

  • A 1st order stream has no tributaries feeding into it.
  • When two streams of the same order meet, a stream of the next order up is formed (two 1st order → one 2nd order; two 2nd order → one 3rd order, etc.)
  • If a lower-order stream joins a higher-order stream, the higher order is unchanged.
Laws of stream order
  • More streams of a lower order than higher order.
  • The lower the order, the steeper the gradient.
  • The lower the order, the shorter the stream length.
  • The lower the order, the smaller the drainage basin.

River discharge & flow types

River discharge — the volume of water flowing down a river, measured in cumecs (cubic metres per second).

Laminar flow

Water moves in smooth, parallel sheets with very little friction — found where the riverbed is smooth (e.g. on a floodplain). Speeds can actually be great because there's little turbulence to slow the water.

Turbulent flow

Water moves unevenly, with eddies, bubbles and white water, caused by friction over a rocky, uneven bed and steep gradient — typical of the upper course.

River Course & Erosion Processes

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

Exam pattern"Provide THREE pieces of evidence that this river is in its upper course" is a recurring question (2024 Trials). Always pair each piece of evidence with a brief reason, e.g. "boulders present → indicates mountainous, upper-course terrain".

Erosion processes

  • Hydraulic action — the sheer force of moving water loosening and removing material from the bed and banks.
  • Abrasion / corrasion — rock fragments carried by the river grind and wear away the bed and banks.
  • Attrition — rock particles collide with each other, breaking down into smaller, rounder fragments.
  • Corrosion / solution — chemical dissolving of soluble minerals in the rock by the river water.

Transportation processes

  • Traction — large boulders rolled/dragged along the riverbed (upper course).
  • Saltation — smaller stones bounced/hopped along the bed.
  • Suspension — fine silt and clay particles carried within the water column.
  • Solution — dissolved minerals carried invisibly in the water.

River Features & Meanders

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

Depositional features

  • Levee — as a river floods, coarser/heavier material is deposited closest to the bank first, building up a raised natural embankment over time.
  • Floodplain — the flat land bordering a river, built up from repeated deposits of fine, fertile alluvium during flood events. Very good for farming: rich alluvial soil, close to water for irrigation, flat and easy to work, open with plenty of sun.

Meanders & oxbow lakes

Meanders form through a combination of erosion on the outer bank (faster flow, deeper water) and deposition on the inner bank (slower flow, shallower water — forms a point bar/slip-off slope).

  • As meanders migrate, the neck of the loop narrows.
  • During a flood, the river cuts straight across the neck, abandoning the loop.
  • The abandoned loop becomes an oxbow lake.
  • Once it dries out completely, it becomes a meander scar.
Related termIncised meander: forms when a meandering river is rejuvenated (gains energy) and starts eroding vertically down into its bed instead of laterally, cutting a meander shape deep into the landscape (e.g. Horse-shoe Bend, Kowie River).

Base Levels & Rejuvenation

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

Base levels

Base level of erosion — the lowest level to which a river can erode.

  • Permanent base level — the sea (the ultimate base level for any river).
  • Temporary base level — anything that temporarily interrupts a river's profile: a lake, a dam, a resistant rock layer, a waterfall.

A river profile with temporary base levels is ungraded (not a smooth concave curve). A fully graded river has a smooth, concave longitudinal profile down to its permanent base level.

Exam pattern"Draw a longitudinal profile and label TWO temporary base levels and ONE permanent base level" appears almost every year (2024, 2025 Trials). Practice sketching a profile with dips/steps at dams or resistant rock, ending at sea level.

Rejuvenation

Rejuvenation occurs when a river's speed and erosive power increase, causing it to erode a new valley into an old one. Causes include:

  • A worldwide drop in sea level (e.g. onset of an ice age)
  • Uplift of the land by internal geologic forces (isostasy)
  • A significant increase in rainfall
  • A fast tributary joining the main river
  • After river capture has occurred

Features resulting from rejuvenation:

  • Knickpoint — a sharp break in the river's profile where the new (lower) erosion level intersects the old one; often a waterfall. It slowly migrates upstream (headward erosion) until the profile smooths out again.
  • River terraces — when a river in a wide valley is rejuvenated, it carves a new, narrower valley into its own old floodplain, leaving flat "steps" on either side.
  • Incised meanders — a meandering river gains energy and cuts down (vertically) faster than its valley widens, preserving the meander shape as a deep gorge.

Waterfalls

  • Formed where a river crosses from a resistant (hard) rock layer onto a softer layer.
  • The soft rock is undercut faster than the hard rock, forming a plunge pool and an overhang.
  • As the overhang collapses repeatedly, the waterfall retreats upstream — this is headward erosion.
  • A waterfall acts as a temporary base level and is typically found in the upper course.

River Capture (Stream Piracy)

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

River capture happens when a more powerful, faster-eroding stream erodes headward through a watershed (this process is called abstraction) and "captures" the flow of a neighbouring, less powerful stream.

Why one stream captures another:

  • It may be on the windward side of the watershed, receiving more rainfall and therefore more erosive energy.
  • It may have a steeper gradient, giving it more energy.
  • It may be eroding through weaker or more fractured rock.

Resulting features:

  • Elbow of capture — the sharp bend where the capturing stream intercepts the captured one.
  • Misfit stream — the remaining downstream section of the captured river, now carrying far less water than its valley suggests it once did.
  • Wind gap — the dry, gravel-floored valley left behind between the elbow of capture and the misfit stream.
The capturing river is rejuvenated by the extra volume of water — this links river capture directly to the rejuvenation topic above.

Catchment & River Management

Paper 1 · Section B: Fluvial Processes

Human impacts on drainage basins

  • Pollution — chemical (pesticides, industrial chemicals) and physical (oil runoff from urban roads, heavy metals).
  • Siltation — build-up of silt/clay in the water, reducing water depth, lowering dam capacity, and making the gradient gentler (energy loss).
  • Eutrophication — excess nutrients (often from fertiliser runoff and waste) cause algae and fungi to overgrow, depleting oxygen in the water.

Preserving & managing drainage basins

  • Remove alien vegetation — invasive plants use excessive water, reducing stream flow by an estimated 10%.
  • Inter-basin transfer schemes — move water from wetter to drier basins to support agriculture and urban use.
  • Manage groundwater supply — prevents overuse that lowers the water table.
  • Recycle water — reduces demand for new water. Pretoria/Johannesburg reuse around 50% of urban and industrial water; Cape Town/Durban reuse roughly 5–15%.
  • Conserve wetlands — reed beds naturally filter out toxins and silt; wetland loss to development reduces fresh water supply.
  • Preserve vegetation cover — slows runoff, increases infiltration, reduces soil erosion and siltation of dams.
  • Better farming practices — controlled fertiliser use, re-vegetation programmes.
  • Upgrade and maintain sewage systems — reduces pollution input.
VerifyThe 10% figure (alien vegetation) and the 50% / 5–15% reuse figures came from your own notes rather than a citable published source I can verify independently — treat them as approximately correct and usable for exam purposes, but don't stake a general-knowledge claim on them outside this context.

Riparian vegetation

  • Reduces riverbank erosion — root systems hold soil in place.
  • Improves water quality — acts as a natural filter for pollutants and silt.

Mid-Latitude Cyclones (MLCs)

Paper 1 · Section A: Climate

Formation — four stages

  • Initial stage — a front (boundary) separates warm and cold air masses; the two masses slide past each other, and their interaction causes condensation and precipitation along the boundary.
  • Mature stage — warm air pushes into the low-pressure area left by the retreating cold air; a well-developed warm sector forms between the two fronts.
  • Occlusion stage — the denser cold front catches up with the (slower) warm front, lifting the warm air off the ground and forming an occluded front.
  • Dissipating stage — the cold front completely overtakes the warm front; the supply of warm, moist air is cut off and the system weakens.

Characteristics & movement

  • Winds spiral clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere (Coriolis effect).
  • MLCs move from west to east, carried along in the westerly wind belt.
  • They bring high wind speeds and rainfall, especially to coastal regions.

Weather sequence as an MLC passes over

StageWindTemperatureRainfallClouds
Ahead of warm frontNELow, gradually risingSteady, continuousNimbostratus, altostratus
Warm sector (between fronts)N / NWHighLight or noneMore settled, some sun

Exam pattern"Account for the changes in wind direction / temperature as a mid-latitude cyclone makes landfall" is asked almost every year — answer in sequence (ahead of front → warm sector → cold front) rather than just describing one moment.

Related terms

  • Occluded front / occlusion — where the cold front has fully overtaken the warm front, lifting all the warm air off the surface.
  • Family of cyclones — the term for when two or more MLCs join together along the same frontal boundary.
  • Cut-off low — an isolated pocket of cold, unstable upper-air low pressure that has become cut off from the main westerly flow; more intense than a normal frontal system because it stalls in place, dumping sustained heavy rain (can cause flooding, and in the interior, snow).
  • Warm fronts rarely have a strong effect on South African weather — by the time systems reach us, occlusion has often already occurred, so we mostly feel cold front effects.

South Africa — summer conditions

  • Thermal low forms over the heated interior — rising, unstable air produces Highveld thunderstorms.
  • Tropical cyclones occur off the east coast (sea temperatures are at their warmest).
  • South Atlantic & South Indian high-pressure cells shift further south (following the ITCZ), which tends to block MLCs from reaching the country.
  • Temperatures generally warmer countrywide.

South Africa — winter conditions

  • Kalahari high forms over the interior — cool, sinking air brings stable, dry weather.
  • MLCs become more frequent, bringing rain to the west coast.
  • High-pressure cells shift further north, allowing MLCs to reach further into the continent.
  • Cut-off lows and coastal lows are common, bringing heavy rain or even snow.

Named local systems

  • Cape Doctor — a strong, dry south-easterly wind over Cape Town, created by ridging of the South Atlantic high-pressure system along the coast.
Watch thisYour own Trials paper tests the Cape Doctor as being created by ridging of the South Atlantic high (not South Indian) — double-check this against your teacher's version since sources vary slightly on which high dominates.

Tropical Cyclones

Paper 1 · Section A: Climate

Conditions needed for formation

  • Sea surface temperature of 27°C or above (warm ocean = energy source).
  • Located between roughly 5° and 30° latitude — far enough from the equator for the Coriolis force to create rotation, but not so far that it's too cool.
  • Clear, relatively windless upper-atmosphere conditions, allowing an intense low pressure system to organise without being sheared apart.

Evidence of a mature system

  • A clearly visible eye at the centre of the storm.
  • Large-scale spiralling cumulonimbus cloud bands.
  • The system has been formally named and classified (e.g. given a category).

Impacts & tracking

  • Storm surge — gale-force winds push a mound of sea water ahead of the storm onto the coast, flooding low-lying coastal areas.
  • Tropical cyclones are tracked using polar-orbiting satellites rather than geostationary ones, because polar orbiters pass much closer to the surface and can capture higher-resolution detail as they cross the storm.
  • Typical path: cyclones generally track from east to west initially (steered by the trade/tropical easterlies), then curve poleward as they lose their easterly steering influence.

Valley Climates

Paper 1 · Section A: Climate

Mountain & valley winds

  • Katabatic / mountain winds — at night, air in contact with the valley sides cools, becomes denser, and sinks down the full length of the valley to the floor.
  • Anabatic / valley winds — during the day, the valley sides heat up faster than the valley floor; warm air rises up the slopes.
  • Valley (temperature) inversion — cold, dense air sinks and pools at the valley floor at night, sitting below a layer of warmer air — the reverse of the normal temperature pattern. Needs clear, calm conditions to form (no cloud cover to trap heat, no wind to mix the air).
  • Thermal belt — the zone partway up the valley side where temperatures are warmest at night, because the coldest air has drained further down and settled on the floor.
  • Frost pocket — where temperatures at the valley floor drop below 0°C at night; the coldest, lowest point in the valley.

Frost & farming

  • Frost is most common in winter, on clear, windless nights (the same conditions needed for a temperature inversion).
  • Ice/frost forms when the dew point drops below 0°C and water droplets on surfaces freeze.
  • Large diameter fans prevent frost by mechanically mixing the air — stopping cold air from settling and stagnating at ground level, so the dew point at crop height doesn't reach freezing.

Aspect

Aspect — the direction a slope faces, which controls how much direct sun it receives.

In the Southern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes receive more direct sunlight (face the equator) and are warmer, with a smaller shadow zone. South-facing slopes are cooler, shadier, and have a larger shadow zone.

Urban Climates

Paper 1 · Section A: Climate

Urban heat island

Cities are consistently warmer than surrounding rural areas — most noticeable at night.

  • Concrete, tar, and building materials absorb heat during the day and release it slowly overnight, keeping night-time temperatures higher and giving the CBD a smaller diurnal temperature range than a vegetated area (e.g. CBD 24°C day / 16°C night = 8°C range, vs a park at 22°C / 11°C = 11°C range).
  • Vegetated areas cool down faster at night since they don't retain heat the way hard surfaces do — giving them a larger diurnal range.
Exam pattern"Compare, then account for" diurnal range questions are standard — always give the CBD's smaller range as a fact first, then explain using heat absorption/retention of urban surfaces.

Night (temperature) inversion & pollution dome

  • Night inversion — the term for why CBD temperatures stay warmer at night (heat retained by concrete/tar radiating back out).
  • Pollution dome — at night the air over a city cools and becomes denser, sinking and compacting; this traps pollutants close to the ground in a dome shape over the CBD.

Green zones & mitigation

Green zones (parks, gardens, reserves) moderate city climate by absorbing CO₂, providing shade, boosting O₂ levels, and offering recreation space.

Other methods to curb the urban heat island effect:

  • Sustainable energy systems & reduced carbon emissions
  • Increased public transport (fewer vehicle emissions)
  • Painting buildings white/pale to increase the albedo effect (more heat reflected)
  • Rooftop gardens
  • Afforestation / tree planting

Settlement Hierarchy

Paper 1 · Section C: Settlement

What is it?

The arrangement of settlements from smallest to largest, based on population size, range/type of services, sphere of influence (hinterland), and economic activities.

Moving up the hierarchy: population increases, services become more specialised, threshold increases (more people needed to support the service), and frequency of visits decreases.

The levels

LevelCharacteristicsTypical services
Isolated farmsteadOne household; no services; tiny sphere of influenceNone
HamletVery small cluster of houses; often at a crossroadsMaybe one shop or church
Village / low-order centreBasic, everyday services; small hinterland; visited frequentlySpaza shop, primary school, clinic, petrol pump
Minor country townBasic + some specialised services; hub for surrounding villagesSupermarket, high school, police station, bank
Major regional townSignificant population; industrial activity; services a whole districtHospitals, government offices, shopping centres
CityVery large population; CBD, suburbs, industry; major employerUniversities, airports, major hospitals

Threshold & range

  • Threshold population — the minimum number of people needed to support a service. Low-order service (spaza shop) = low threshold; high-order service (specialist hospital) = high threshold.
  • Range of a service — the maximum distance people are willing to travel for it. Low-order = short range; high-order = long range.
  • Sphere of influence / hinterland — the area served by a settlement. Grows with settlement size.

Rural Settlement

Paper 1 · Section C: Settlement

Describing a settlement

  • Site — the actual physical land the settlement sits on. Determined by aspect, relief, natural resources, shelter (bay/inlet), and water availability (a wet-point site has reliable water; a dry-point site avoids flooding, e.g. built above a floodplain).
  • Situation — the location relative to surrounding features: physical features (mountains, coast), neighbouring settlements, communication links (highways, rivers).
  • Function — the settlement's main economic/social activities.
  • Shape — its physical layout pattern.

Settlement patterns

Nucleated (clustered)Dispersed (scattered)
DescriptionHouses built closely together in a small areaHouses spread widely apart (typical of farm homesteads)
AdvantagesSocial interaction, efficient shared services, good transport links, safety in numbers, help always availableMore space, freedom, quieter, less pollution
DisadvantagesNoisy, less space, more pollutionFar from services, less social interaction, expensive transport

Shape terms (used to describe rural settlement layout): linear, cross-roads, round, T-junction, star-shaped.

Farming types

  • Subsistence farming — small scale, produces mainly for the family, small surplus for income.
  • Commercial farming — run as a business for profit, uses modern technology and machinery; can be small-scale/intensive (e.g. market gardening) or large-scale/extensive.

Forces & rural development

  • Centrifugal forces — factors that push people away from an area (droughts, other natural disasters, farm attacks, land reform uncertainty, unproductive soil).
  • Centripetal forces — factors that draw people toward an area (new infrastructure, economic growth, employment opportunities).
  • Farm diversification — farmers add income sources beyond one crop (e.g. agritourism, wine tasting) so that if one crop or season fails, other income continues; different harvest times keep cash flow steady year-round.

Rural development strategies (to reduce rural-urban migration):

  • Basic needs strategy — providing essential services: water, electricity, sanitation.
  • Ecotourism / special events — draws visitor spending into the local economy, creates jobs.
  • Industrialisation / decentralisation — relocating industry into rural areas for jobs and infrastructure investment.
  • New Ruralism — a movement/trend of people actively choosing to relocate to rural towns (often linked to remote work, lifestyle, or a specific town's growth, e.g. Nongoma).
  • Sustainability practices, e.g. Agenda 21 principles.

Urban Settlement

Paper 1 · Section C: Settlement

CBD & land values

Property prices are highest in the CBD because land is limited and in high demand — the CBD has the greatest concentration of businesses and services, so competition for space drives prices up.

Inner city problems: traffic congestion, high pollution, crime (highest population concentration), urban decay (graffiti, slum formation).

Gentrification & renewal

  • Gentrification — upgrading/redeveloping older inner-city buildings and areas, attracting wealthier residents and businesses back into the CBD — vital for inner-city renewal, reviving investment and infrastructure.
  • Drawback — can price out lower-income original residents, displacing established communities (a key socio-economic cost to weigh against the benefits).

Township issues & redevelopment

Traditional South African townships often face poor service delivery: bad road infrastructure, limited electricity and water supply, sporadic refuse removal, poor sanitation, and few schools/hospitals/retail options.

Township redevelopment (e.g. shopping malls, transport upgrades) matters because it brings services and jobs directly into townships, reducing the need for residents to migrate elsewhere for opportunity — directly addressing rural/township-to-city migration pressure.

Centralisation vs decentralisation

Problems with centralisation of industry (many industries clustered together):

  • Pollution — concentrated factories become a health hazard (e.g. Sasolburg, Wentworth).
  • Overcrowding — rural-urban migration in search of jobs.
  • Localised unemployment once an area is oversupplied with job-seekers.

Government response — decentralisation, promoted through Special Economic Zones (SEZs) / Industrial Development Zones (IDZs), using tax rebates, free skills training, and cheaper land.

Eastern Cape Industrial Region

Paper 1 · Section C: Economic Geography of South Africa

Core industrial areas

Coega SEZ (near Gqeberha) and the East London Industrial Development Zone (ELIDZ) anchor the province's economy. Main activity: motor vehicle assembly (Mercedes-Benz — East London; Volkswagen — Kariega/Uitenhage; Isuzu — Gqeberha), vehicle parts, and agro-processing.

Factors favouring development

  • Ports — Ngqura, Gqeberha, and East London give access to international trade; Ngqura is one of SA's deepest, most modern ports.
  • SEZ incentives — Coega and ELIDZ offer world-class infrastructure and investment incentives.
  • Automotive strength — SA's leading vehicle-manufacturing region, drawing supporting/link industries.
  • Labour — large workforce, supported by universities and TVET colleges.
  • Agriculture — fertile land and climate support dairy, citrus, wool, forestry — feeding agro-processing industries.
  • Tourism — Addo Elephant Park, Wild Coast, cultural heritage sites bring jobs and income.
  • Renewable energy — strong wind/solar resources attract green energy investment.

Factors hindering development

  • High unemployment and poverty, reducing local purchasing power.
  • Skills shortages in specialised technical/engineering roles.
  • Inadequate infrastructure outside the main SEZ nodes (roads, rail, water, connectivity).
  • Load shedding/electricity supply problems raising production costs.
  • Distance from major markets (Gauteng, Cape Town) increases transport costs.
  • Water shortages from periodic drought affecting industry and agriculture.
  • Limited economic diversification — heavy reliance on the automotive sector, and difficulty competing with cheaper Asian/Eastern European labour costs.

Gold Mining in South Africa

Factors favouring gold mining

  • Rich deposits — the Witwatersrand Basin holds some of the largest gold reserves ever discovered.
  • Advanced technology — sophisticated techniques allow extraction from very deep deposits.
  • Infrastructure — strong road, rail, port, financial and communication networks.
  • Labour — large historical workforce; mining towns developed around goldfields.
  • Capital access — local and international investment funds deep-level operations.
  • Market demand — jewellery, investment/banking, electronics, industrial uses keep demand constant.
  • Skills & expertise — long mining history built up engineers, geologists, and skilled miners.
  • Contributes significantly to export earnings, employment, and GDP.

Challenges

  • Very deep mines mean high underground temperatures.
  • High operating costs and safety risks for miners.
  • Environmental issues — water pollution, mine waste/tailings.
  • Declining gold reserves in some established mining areas.

Location

South Africa's deepest gold mines are mainly located in the Witwatersrand Basin, spanning Gauteng and parts of the Free State. Process begins with prospecting and exploration, where geologists search for gold-bearing rock formations before mining begins.

Map code (map referencing system)

South Africa's topographical maps are divided into blocks of 1° × 1°, referenced first by latitude, then longitude — measured from the Equator (0° N/S) and the Greenwich Meridian (0° E/W). South Africa sits in the southern and eastern hemispheres, so all references read °S and °E.

A map code such as 3325CB breaks down as:

  • 33 = 33° South
  • 25 = 25° East
  • C = the big quarter-block C (within the 1°×1° square)
  • B = the small quarter-block B (within big block C)
Exam tipWhen asked to "draw a quadrant reference," always draw the two nested squares, label the latitude/longitude on the outer edges, and shade the correct small block — a bare set of letters without the diagram usually loses marks.

Coordinates (latitude & longitude)

A grid reference gives a location using degrees, minutes, and seconds:

  • 1 degree (1°) = 60 minutes (60')
  • 1 minute (1') = 60 seconds (60")

Working out seconds by measurement:

  • Latitude — measure the distance from the top of the block down to the feature, divide by the full length of the block, then multiply by 60. E.g. (27 mm / 43 mm) × 60 = 38" South.
  • Longitude — measure the distance across from the start (left) of the block to the feature, divide by the block's full width, then multiply by 60. E.g. (20 mm / 37 mm) × 60 = 32" East.

Quick estimation method: since each minute has 60 seconds, a block can be mentally split into quarters of 15" each, allowing an approximate reading without measuring — e.g. a point roughly ⅔ of the way across gives an estimate of about 40".

Common mistakeLatitude is measured top-to-bottom (rows), longitude is measured left-to-right (columns) — mixing these up is one of the most frequent errors students make under exam pressure. Always write "S" for latitude and "E" for longitude in your final answer.

Scale, Distance & Area

Types of scale

TypeLooks likeMeaning

Representative fraction / ratio1 : 50 0001 unit on the map = 50 000 of the same unit in reality
Word scale"1 cm represents 50 000 cm"Same ratio, written in words

Map scale rule: the smaller the number in the ratio, the larger the scale and the more detail shown over a smaller area (e.g. 1:25 000). The bigger the number, the smaller the scale and less detail over a larger area (e.g. 1:100 000; a world map might be 1:2 500 000).

Key conversions

  • 10 mm = 1 cm
  • 1 m = 100 cm
  • 1 km = 1000 m = 100 000 cm

Most topographical maps use 1 : 50 000 — every 1 cm on the map = 50 000 cm in reality = 500 m (½ km).

To findFormula
Distance in kmmeasured cm × 0.5
Distance in mmeasured cm × 500

Travelling distance (along a winding road, railway, or river): lay a piece of string along the route from start to end point, mark the string at the endpoint, then measure the string against a ruler and convert using the table above.

Worked example: 6.4 cm measured → 6.4 × 0.5 = 3.2 km, or 6.4 × 500 = 3200 m.

Calculating area

Area = Length × Breadth, converted for the map's scale:

UnitFormula
Kilometres(Length × 0.5) × (Breadth × 0.5) = km²
Metres(Length × 500) × (Breadth × 500) = m²
HectaresCalculate in m², then divide by 10 000 (1 hectare = 10 000 m²)

Worked example (golf course measuring 3.6 cm × 2.4 cm):

  • km: (3.6 × 0.5) × (2.4 × 0.5) = 1.8 km × 1.2 km = 2.16 km²
  • m: (3.6 × 500) × (2.4 × 500) = 1800 m × 1200 m = 2 160 000 m²
  • hectares: 2 160 000 ÷ 10 000 = 216 ha

Area of an irregular shape

Method 1 — regular shapes: divide the irregular area into squares, triangles, and rectangles; calculate each separately and add them together.

Method 2 — grid squares:

  • Draw a 2 cm × 2 cm grid over the area.
  • At 1:50 000, each 2 cm × 2 cm square = 1 km × 1 km = 1 km².
  • Count whole squares fully inside the shape, then estimate part-squares by combining fractions of squares along the edge.
  • Total squares × 1 km² = estimated area (note: this method is not 100% accurate, only an estimate).

For a smaller feature, use a finer grid, e.g. 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm = 0.25 km × 0.25 km = 0.0625 km² per square.

Altitude, Contours & Landforms

How height is shown on a map

Height is always given in metres above sea level, shown four ways:

  • Contour lines — brown lines joining places of equal height, changing in 20 m intervals. Closer-together contours = steeper change in elevation. The direction the height number is written on a contour shows the direction height increases. Every 5th contour is drawn darker and marks a full "hundred" (e.g. 500 / 600 / 700 m).
  • Benchmark — a fixed point (usually along a road or railway) indicating height above sea level.
  • Spot height — a single point marking the exact height at that specific location.
  • Trigonometrical beacon — shown as a triangle (Δ) with two numbers: the beacon's reference number, and its height in metres below it.

Reading landforms from contours

FeatureWhat it looks like on contours
SpurA high ridge sticking out into a valley; contours bulge outward toward lower ground. Interlocking spurs are common in the upper (mountainous) course of a river.
ValleyA low-lying depression carved by a river; contours bend inward toward higher ground. Vertical erosion in the upper course creates a V-shaped valley.
Saddle / nekA dip or hollow between two higher points/ridges — often acts as a watershed dividing two drainage basins.
CliffWhere two or more contour lines touch or merge — shows a very rapid change in height.
WaterfallA cliff where a river crosses it.
PoortA narrow gap where a river has eroded through a mountain range; very steep sides, so contours are tightly packed on both sides.
Common mistakeDon't just say "the contours are close together" for a cliff — for full marks state that the lines touch or merge, since "close together" alone can also describe a steep slope that isn't a true cliff.

Cross-Sections & Vertical Exaggeration

Paper 2 · Mapwork & GIS Skills

Drawing a cross-section — method

  1. Lay a strip of paper along the line you need (from start point to end point) on the topographical map, and mark off the start/end points plus every contour line it crosses.
  2. Work out the height of each mark — contours change in 20 m intervals, and every 5th line is a full "hundred". Note any major features crossed (roads, rivers, land use).
  3. Set up your graph axes: the horizontal axis length = the length of your marked paper strip. Horizontal scale is always the map's own scale (1:50 000 for a topo map, 1:10 000 for an orthophoto). The vertical scale is normally given (e.g. 1 cm = 20 / 50 / 100 m) — start the vertical axis just below your lowest recorded height.
  4. Lay the paper strip along the horizontal axis and transfer each contour mark up to its correct height as a dot.
  5. Join the dots freehand (not with a ruler) for a realistic, smoothly curved landscape profile. Mark and label any required features.
  6. Label both scales (HS and VS) and give the cross-section a heading, e.g. "Cross-section from point Δ14 to ▪305."
Exam tipAlways join your points with a smooth freehand curve, not straight ruled segments — a "spiky" cross-section loses marks even if every point is plotted correctly.

Vertical exaggeration

Vertical exaggeration (VE) measures how much a cross-section distorts reality by using a taller vertical scale than the horizontal one.

VE = Horizontal Scale (HS) ÷ Vertical Scale (VS)

Worked example — topographical map (HS = 1:50 000), vertical scale given as 1 cm = 20 m:

  • Step 1: convert VS to the same ratio format — 20 m × 100 (cm per m) = 2000 cm, so VS = 1:2000.
  • Step 2: VE = 50 000 ÷ 2000 = 25 times.
ScenarioHSVS givenVS as ratioVE
Topo map, 1cm=50m1:50 0001cm = 50m1:500050 000 ÷ 5000 = 10×
Orthophoto, 1cm=5m1:10 0001cm = 5m1:50010 000 ÷ 500 = 20×
Common mistakeRemember the orthophoto's horizontal scale is 1:10 000, not 1:50 000 — using the wrong HS is the single most common vertical exaggeration error.

Direction, Bearings & Gradient

Paper 2 · Mapwork & GIS Skills

True bearing

True bearing shows direction as a number, measured clockwise from True North (0°, straight up on the map) using a protractor.

Magnetic declination

Magnetic declination calculates how far Magnetic North has shifted since the map was surveyed/drawn.

Worked example:

  • Step 1 — Current year − map's year: 2023 − 2006 = 17 years.
  • Step 2 — Years × annual rate of change: 17 × 6' = 102' = 1° 42'.
  • Step 3 — Add to the map's printed declination: 1° 42' + 22° 44' = 24° 26' West of True North.

Magnetic bearing

Magnetic bearing = True Bearing + Magnetic Declination.

ExampleTrue bearingDeclinationMagnetic bearing
1124°24° 26' W of TN124° + 24° 26' = 148° 26'

Common mistakeOnly add the whole degrees together directly in these examples — don't try to add minutes across different units without keeping track separately, and remember a bearing can never be recorded as 360° or more: if your total passes 359°, subtract 360° and continue from 0°.

Gradient

Gradient compares the change in height between two points to the horizontal distance between them, usually expressed as a ratio 1 : x.

A ratio like 1 : 5.53 means: for every 1 vertical metre climbed, you travel 5.53 horizontal metres. The closer the number is to 1, the steeper the slope — so 1:5 is far steeper than 1:55.

Worked example — gradient from Δ150 (1486.3 m) to Δ153 (844.9 m), measured 7.1 cm apart on a 1:50 000 map:

  • Step 1 — difference in height: 1486.3 − 844.9 = 641.4 m
  • Step 2 — distance between points: 7.1 cm × 500 = 3550 m (always convert to metres to match the height units)
  • Step 3 — divide distance by height: 3550 ÷ 641.4 = 5.53
  • Gradient = 1 : 5.53

What is GIS?

A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a system designed to capture, store, manipulate, analyse, manage, and present all types of geographical data.

Aerial photograph types

High oblique~60°Covers a large area; the horizon is visible in the image

Other image types

  • False colour image — depicts objects in colours different from a true-colour photograph (often used to highlight vegetation health, water, etc.).
  • Thematic image — shows the geographic pattern of one particular subject (e.g. temperature, language, population) using map symbols for properties not naturally visible.

Remote sensing

Remote sensing — detecting and monitoring an area's physical characteristics by measuring reflected/emitted radiation from a distance (typically satellite or aircraft), without physical contact. It picks up wavelengths across the electromagnetic spectrum, not just the visible light humans see.

Why it's used: information is received quickly; relatively cheap; data can be manipulated for more informed decisions.

Resolution

Resolution — the number of pixels across an image's width and height. More pixels = greater clarity = higher resolution; fewer/bigger pixels = less detail = lower resolution. Small objects show more detail in high-resolution images.

Data storage: spatial, attribute, vector & raster

  • Spatial data — provides a location (e.g. coordinates).
  • Attribute data — describes the characteristics of a feature (e.g. for a road: length, number of lanes, number of tolls; for a farm: name, crop type, farming type, size).
  • Vector data — the most common storage format, built from points, then lines, then polygons (areas/shapes), added to GIS software in that order.
  • Raster data — displayed as a grid of pixelated images; any digital image is raster data. Pixel count determines clarity.

Data manipulation & layering

  • Data layering — showing different vector/raster datasets as separate layers, each representing one theme, so they can be analysed in isolation or combined.
  • Querying — manipulating GIS data to answer geographical questions.
  • Data integration — combining data from different sources into one unified map using shared spatial location.
  • Buffering — identifying zones at set distances from a feature, e.g. a noise buffer next to a road, a safety buffer around a hazard, or a flood-risk buffer around a wetland/marsh.

Orthophoto Skills

Working with orthophotos

Orthophotos are vertical aerial photographs, using a scale of 1 : 10 000 — 1 cm represents 10 000 cm, or 100 m, in reality. To calculate distance: measure in centimetres and multiply by 100.

Worked example — distance from spot height 616 (point A) to spot height 496 (point B), measured 7.3 cm apart:

  • Distance = 7.3 × 100 = 730 m

Worked example — gradient between the same two points:

  • Change in height: 616 − 496 = 120 m
  • Distance: 7.3 × 100 = 730 m
  • Gradient = 730 ÷ 120 = 1 : 6.08
Common mistakeEvery orthophoto calculation uses ×100 for distance (scale 1:10 000) — using the topographical map's ×500 conversion (1:50 000) by habit is the single most common orthophoto error.

Identifying features

When asked to identify land use or features on an orthophoto, cross-reference the same area on the accompanying topographical map extract — shared landmarks (roads, rivers, distinctive shapes) help confirm what you're looking at.

Typical identifications include: recreational grounds, golf courses, schools, and forestry/holiday resorts — identified by their distinctive shapes (e.g. a golf course's fairways/greens, a school's field and building layout).
